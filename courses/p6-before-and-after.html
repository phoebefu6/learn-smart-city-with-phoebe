Part 0

Wrenmouth's director wants a headline

Wrenmouth is a constructed transport office in a fictional city. Its team ranked junctions by last month's delays, retimed the signals at the five worst, measured again a month later, and the five are better. The director has a headline drafted. The analyst's job tonight is not to say no; it is to say which other stories fit the same two numbers, and what it would take to tell them apart.

Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class 🔍 Every diagram zooms

★ What you walk out with tonight. Five rival stories you can say out loud against any before-after: the change, regression to the mean, season and calendar, something else that changed, and a measure that moved. The mechanism of regression to the mean in one picture: pick on a noisy baseline, re-measure, and the picks drift back toward the average with no treatment. A seeded Python simulation that shows it, shows it growing with measurement error, and shows what a comparison group picked the same way buys you. And a test of the same mechanism on real taxi rows. Estimating the effect properly is learn experimentation's job; you leave knowing when to call it.

LiveThe slide that starts every argument2 min▶

A before-after has three parts: a window before, a change, a window after. It is the cheapest evaluation there is and the one every city can afford, which is why it is everywhere. It answers "did the number move?" well. It answers "did our change move it?" only when every other reason the number could move has been ruled out, and one site with one before and one after rarely rules out anything.

That is not a reason to distrust the team. It is a reason to write "after the change, delays fell" instead of "the change cut delays" until the design earns the stronger sentence.

The estimation methods themselves, difference-in-differences and synthetic control, are taught properly in learn experimentation, session b9 and session b8. Part 1 · covers before-after as a claim, and the contested evidence

A before-after is a claim, not a finding

12 min live

Draw the director's slide and then, underneath it, list every story that would produce exactly that drop. If more than one fits, the slide does not choose between them. The skill is to make the list quickly and calmly, before anyone is attached to the first story.

LiveFive stories, said out loud5 min▶

The change did it. The story on the slide. It may well be true. It is the only one of the five the team wants, which is the reason to list the others first.

Back to normal. Wrenmouth picked the five junctions because last month they were the worst. Some of that badness was the junction; some was a bad month. Measure again and the bad-month part does not repeat. Part 2 is all about this one.

Season and calendar. A month with school holidays, a month with more rain, a month with an extra Friday. Trip counts and delays follow the calendar whether or not anything changed.

Something else changed too. A fare change, a closed bridge, a new rule across the whole city. Concurrent changes land on the same dates as yours and get credited to yours.

The measure moved. A new sensor model, a recalibration, a changed definition of "delay", a column that did not exist before. If the ruler changed, the number can move with nothing on the street moving.

LivePilot results are the commonest case4 min▶

"The pilot cut journey times" is a sentence you will read often. A single-site before-after is a claim, not a finding: it fits the change, and it fits every story in the figure. Pilots are especially exposed to "back to normal", because pilot sites are rarely chosen at random. They are chosen because they were the worst, the most complained about, or the most visible, and each of those is a selection on a high baseline.

The honest rewrite keeps the observation and drops the verb: "after the pilot started, journey times at the site fell; we have not yet ruled out regression to the mean, season or concurrent changes." That sentence is publishable today. The stronger one needs a design.

Real world

The measure can move under you even in a well-run public file. The TLC data dictionary adds a column, cbd_congestion_fee, a "Per-trip charge for MTA's Congestion Relief Zone starting Jan. 5, 2025." The October 2024 file used across these sessions does not have it at all. Anyone comparing totals across that date compares two schemas as well as two periods: check what a total includes before and after before comparing it.

Self-studyCongestion pricing: where reasonable people disagree3 min▶

Road-use charges are the before-after argument at city scale. Evaluations disagree on how large the effects are, how long they last and who bears the cost. Some readers see a durable change in traffic and travel; others see effects that fade, shift to other places and times, or fall hardest on people with the fewest alternatives. Both positions are held by careful people looking at real data.

Part of why they disagree is exactly tonight's list: before-after comparisons of a charge are exposed to regression to the mean, to season, and to everything else that changed in the same year. This page gives no figure from any city's scheme, and does not pick a side. What you can do is ask, of any number you are shown: which of the five stories did this evaluation rule out, and how? The methods that rule them out are in learn experimentation b9 and b8.

Part 2 · covers regression to the mean (Barnett et al. 2005)

Pick the worst, measure again, watch them improve

13 min live

Regression to the mean is the story most likely to be behind a city pilot, because city teams naturally act where things look worst. The mechanism is simple enough to draw: rank sites on one noisy measurement, take the worst, measure them again, and they drift back toward the average with nothing done to them.

LiveWhat the epidemiologists wrote down5 min▶

Barnett, van der Pols and Dobson wrote the standard short explanation for medicine, and it transfers to streets unchanged. Regression to the mean "can make natural variation in repeated data look like real change". Their rule: "unusually large or small measurements tend to be followed by measurements that are closer to the mean."

Two conditions make it bigger, and a city meets both. The effect grows "with increasing measurement error", and "when follow-up measurements are only examined on a sub-sample selected using a baseline value." Picking the five worst junctions and re-measuring only those five is that sub-sample, exactly.

Their advice is just as plain: it "should always be considered as a possible cause of an observed change", and it is dealt with by design and suitable methods, not by hoping.

LiveWhy thin data makes it worse5 min▶

Measurement error in city data is often just small numbers. A zone with a handful of pickups a day can double or halve from one week to the next by chance. Rank zones by last week's change and the top and bottom of the list fill up with thin zones, because thin zones are where chance moves the number most.

Real world

In the October 2024 taxi records, Staten Island's 20 zones recorded 231 pickups in the whole month, and every one of those zones is under 100 pickups. A "worst week" list built from these records would be dominated by zones where a few trips decide the ranking. Practitioner session 4 shows where the data is this thin; tonight's point is what it does to a before-after: the thinner the cell, the bigger the drift back.

The fix is not to drop thin zones silently. It is to judge them on more than one measurement, or to compare them with similar zones that were not changed.

Self-studyDefences by design, named not taught3 min▶

Measure the baseline more than once. Select on one window, use a second, separate window as the "before". The luck that got a site picked is then not in the number you compare against.

A comparison group picked the same way. Take the worst ten, change five chosen by a seeded random draw, leave five alone, re-measure all ten. Both groups regress; the difference between their changes is the part the luck cannot explain. The build-along simulates this.

The full methods. Difference-in-differences generalises the comparison-group idea: learn experimentation b9. When you have one changed area and many unchanged ones, synthetic control builds a weighted look-alike: learn experimentation b8. For auditing the causal claim itself, learn causal inference a2.

Part 3 · build-along

Simulate the drift, then test it on real rows

17 min hands on

Two halves. First a seeded simulation of 60 junctions where nothing is ever changed, so any improvement you see is regression to the mean and nothing else. Then the same move on the real October 2024 taxi records from practitioner session 1. Every number here is printed by your own run at your settings; your numbers will differ with the seed, and the shape will not. This page prints none of them, on purpose.

Simulate 60 junctions where nothing ever changes

Each junction gets a fixed true average delay, then two noisy measurements a month apart. No treatment exists anywhere in this code, so any improvement you find is the selection talking.

pythonimport numpy as np rng = np.random.default_rng(1017) n = 60 true = rng.normal(10, 2, n) # each junction's real average delay, minutes before = true + rng.normal(0, 3, n) # one noisy month after = true + rng.normal(0, 3, n) # measured again, nothing changed

Pick the worst five and measure them again

Print three numbers: the five's average before, their average after, and the average of all 60 after. The five improve, toward the overall average, with no treatment. Write your three numbers down.

pythonworst = np.argsort(before)[-5:] print(before[worst].mean(), after[worst].mean(), after.mean())

Turn up the measurement error

Barnett and colleagues say the effect grows with measurement error. Check it: rerun the pick at three noise levels and print the apparent improvement each time. Thin city data is the high-noise row.

pythonfor noise in [1, 3, 6]: b = true + rng.normal(0, noise, n) a = true + rng.normal(0, noise, n) w = np.argsort(b)[-5:] print(noise, round(b[w].mean() - a[w].mean(), 2))

Add a comparison group picked the same way

Take the worst ten, change five chosen by a seeded random draw, leave five alone. With no true effect, both groups improve and the difference sits near zero. Then set effect = 1 and rerun: the treated group's raw improvement is usually bigger than 1 (the luck is still in it), while the difference moves toward 1. With five per group the difference is noisy, which is exactly why b9 and b8 exist.

pythoneffect = 0 top10 = np.argsort(before)[-10:] treated = rng.permutation(top10)[:5] control = np.setdiff1d(top10, treated) after2 = after.copy() after2[treated] -= effect gain_t = (before[treated] - after2[treated]).mean() gain_c = (before[control] - after2[control]).mean() print(round(gain_t, 2), round(gain_c, 2), round(gain_t - gain_c, 2))

Test it on real rows: the zones that fell hardest

Use the kept table from practitioner session 1 (pickup time t, zone z). Count pickups per zone in three equal seven-day windows, so the weekday mix is identical. Among zones with at least 100 pickups in week one, take the ten that fell most from week one to week two. Nothing was done to them. Look at week three, and write down whether they bounced back. This page prints no number for it: it is your measurement, and if they did not rebound, list which of the other four stories might hold them down.

pythonimport pandas as pd week = pd.cut(kept.t.dt.day, [0, 7, 14, 21], labels=["w1", "w2", "w3"]) c = kept.groupby([kept.z, week], observed=True).size().unstack() c = c[c.w1 >= 100] fell = (c.w2 / c.w1).nsmallest(10).index print(c.loc[fell, ["w1", "w2", "w3"]].sum())

The habit to keep. Before anyone writes "the change cut", list the five stories and say which ones the design rules out. If you picked sites because they were worst, assume they would have improved anyway until a comparison group picked the same way says otherwise. Write "after", not "because of", until the design earns it.

Check yourself

Three questions

Wrenmouth (constructed) retimed the signals at the five junctions with the worst delays last month. This month all five are better. What is the strongest honest response?

Selecting on a baseline value and re-measuring only the selection is the condition under which regression to the mean is largest (Barnett et al. 2005). A treats consistency as proof; C resolves the question the other way with no more evidence. A comparison group picked the same way separates the two.

When does regression to the mean grow larger?

Barnett and colleagues name both conditions. The build-along shows the first one: raise the noise, and the apparent improvement of the worst five grows. The size of any change made has nothing to do with it; the effect appears with no change at all.

A colleague compares trips into Manhattan's core in October 2024 with February 2025 and writes "the congestion fee reduced trips by this much". What is the right response?

Evaluations of road-use charges disagree on size and durability; this page takes no side and quotes no figure. What it can say is that a two-month before-after leaves season, concurrent changes and a changed measure all standing. The designs that address them are in learn experimentation b9 and b8.

Rewrite one before-after sentence

Find a before-after claim in any public report or article about a city change. Do not name it in your notes; just copy the sentence. Under it, write the five stories, mark which ones the report ruled out and how, and rewrite the sentence so it claims only what the design supports.

Flip the selection
