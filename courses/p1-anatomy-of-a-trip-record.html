LiveRead the dictionary, then the file3 min▶

The TLC publishes a one-page data dictionary for yellow taxi records, dated March 18, 2025. It is good, and it is not the file. The dictionary lists airport_fee; the October 2024 file calls the column Airport_fee, capital A. The dictionary lists cbd_congestion_fee, a charge "starting Jan. 5, 2025"; an October 2024 file does not have it at all.

Real world

The TLC page says the records were "collected and provided to the NYC Taxi and Limousine Commission (TLC) by technology providers", publishes them monthly with about a two-month delay "to allow time for full vendor submissions", and warns that minor schema changes may come. Every one of those sentences is a profiling instruction.

Self-studyGetting the files3 min▶

The TLC Trip Record Data page links one parquet file per month per service: yellow, green and two kinds of for-hire vehicle. It also links the Taxi Zone Lookup table (a small CSV) and the zone shapes. Download into a fresh, empty folder, keep your scripts somewhere else, and read the files; there is nothing in them to run.

The city's Open Data Law lets anyone use public data sets without registration or licence, and lets the city ask republishers to state the source, the version and any changes. Practitioner session 8 turns that into a publishing checklist.

Part 1 · covers the yellow taxi data dictionary

One row, field by field

12 min live

Group the 19 columns by what they measure and the record stops being a wide table and becomes one event: a meter switched on in one zone and off in another. Each group has its own trap, and the one that bites first is the quietest: a column that silently describes only the riders who paid a certain way.

LiveWhat each group measures, in the dictionary's words4 min▶

When. tpep_pickup_datetime is "The date and time when the meter was engaged", and dropoff is when it was disengaged. These are the meter's stamps, not a phone's or a server's. Green records use lpep_ instead of tpep_, so a union of the two needs renamed columns.

Where. PULocationID is the "TLC Taxi Zone in which the taximeter was engaged". A zone is an area with an id, not a point: practitioner session 2 joins it to names and boroughs, and finds the ids that are not places at all.

How far. trip_distance is "The elapsed trip distance in miles reported by the taximeter". In October, 81,639 yellow rows report exactly zero. Some will be cancelled or voided trips; the file does not say which, so keep them and flag them rather than guess.

LiveColumns that describe only some riders4 min▶

The dictionary is blunt: tip_amount "is automatically populated for credit card tips. Cash tips are not included", and total_amount "Does not include cash tips." In October, the payment codes split 2,854,092 credit card, 477,350 cash, 80,888 dispute, 27,546 no charge, and 393,895 rows coded 0, which the dictionary calls a Flex Fare trip.

Those 393,895 code-0 rows are exactly the rows whose store-and-forward flag is empty: the same rows, checked one by one. A column that is null for one group and a code that is zero for the same group is a pattern worth a line in your notes, even before you know why.

Real world

A dashboard that reports "average tip by neighbourhood" from this column reports where card payers tip. If cash use differs between neighbourhoods, the map shows payment habits and calls them generosity. The fix is not a cleverer average; it is a footnote saying whose tips are in the column.

Self-studyProvider codes, and why pages here never name a provider3 min▶

VendorID is "A code indicating the TPEP provider that provided the record." The dictionary maps the codes to company names. For analysis the code is what matters: a defect that appears in one provider's rows and not another's is a provenance finding, and you can report it by code.

Splitting a profile by provider code is the cheapest provenance check there is. If one code accounts for most of the zero distances, or all of the out-of-month stamps, you have found where the problem enters, not just that it exists.

Part 2 · covers how a record reaches the monthly file

A monthly file is not a clean month

10 min live

A trip record makes a journey before it reaches you: the meter stamps it, the vehicle may hold it if it has no connection, the provider's server collects it, the provider submits it, and the TLC publishes the month about two months later. Every hand-off is a place where a stamp can be wrong or a row can arrive in the wrong file. The October files show the result without explaining it.

LiveRows stamped outside the month4 min▶

In the October 2024 yellow file, the earliest pickup stamp is 2009-01-01 00:35:59 and the latest is 2024-11-14 18:30:00. Across yellow and green together, 47 rows fall outside October. That is a tiny share of 3.9 million, and it is still a decision: keep them and your hour-of-week counts include a 2009 Thursday; drop them silently and nobody can reproduce your total.

The professional move is the boring one: filter on the pickup stamp, count what you dropped, and write the count next to the result. The October totals quoted across all sixteen sessions are after exactly this filter.

LiveZones that are not places3 min▶

The zone lookup has 265 rows. Zone 1 is Newark Airport, outside the city. Zone 264 is "Unknown". Zone 265 is "Outside of NYC". In October, 13,169 in-month pickups carry one of those three, and they cannot be placed in any of the five boroughs. Wrenmouth's universe is the 262 zones that are.

A LocationID of 264 is a value in an integer column that means "missing". Treat it as missing, count it, and do not let it become a bar on a map labelled Unknown.

Self-studyWhat the publisher does and does not promise3 min▶

Two sentences frame every use of these files. The TLC: "The trip data was not created by the TLC, and TLC makes no representations as to the accuracy of these data." The city's general terms of use: materials are provided "AS IS", without warranties of accuracy, completeness or currency.

Neither sentence says the data is bad. Together they say the checks are yours. A profiling pass is not optional hygiene here; it is the only quality control between the meter and your chart. For sensor feeds you own, missingness has mechanics of its own: learn IoT data, leader session 2 covers them.

Part 3 · build-along

Profile the month in pandas, and keep a ledger

20 min hands on

Download the October 2024 yellow and green files and the zone lookup into one empty folder, then work from a script kept somewhere else. Each step prints a number; every number below came from running exactly this code with pandas 2.1.4. The figure is the ledger you are building: what came in, what left, and why.

Load both files and look at the schema

You should see 3833771 and 56147, and 19 yellow columns ending in Airport_fee, capital A. Compare that list with the dictionary before anything else.

pythonimport pandas as pd y = pd.read_parquet("yellow_tripdata_2024-10.parquet") g = pd.read_parquet("green_tripdata_2024-10.parquet") print(len(y), len(g)) print(y.columns.tolist())

Profile the columns that only describe some riders

Payment codes come out as 2854092 card, 477350 cash, 393895 code 0, 80888 dispute, 27546 no charge. Then the empty flags and the zero distances: 393895 and 81639.

pythonprint(y["payment_type"].value_counts()) print(y["store_and_fwd_flag"].isna().sum(), (y["trip_distance"] == 0).sum()) print((y["store_and_fwd_flag"].isna() == (y["payment_type"] == 0)).all())

Stack yellow and green, then filter the month

The two services name their timestamps differently, so rename on the way in. The filter drops 47 rows; print the number, do not just apply it.

pythont = pd.concat([ y[["tpep_pickup_datetime", "PULocationID"]].set_axis(["t", "z"], axis=1), g[["lpep_pickup_datetime", "PULocationID"]].set_axis(["t", "z"], axis=1), ]) print(y["tpep_pickup_datetime"].min(), y["tpep_pickup_datetime"].max()) inmonth = (t.t >= "2024-10-01") & (t.t < "2024-11-01") print((~inmonth).sum())

Keep the zones that are places

The lookup has 265 rows and 262 in the five boroughs. You should keep 3876702 pickups and drop 13169. Those are the totals every later session uses.

pythonzones = pd.read_csv("taxi_zone_lookup.csv") five = zones[zones.Borough.isin(["Manhattan", "Brooklyn", "Queens", "Bronx", "Staten Island"])] print(len(zones), len(five)) kept = t[inmonth & t.z.isin(five.LocationID)] print(len(kept), (inmonth & ~t.z.isin(five.LocationID)).sum())

Write the ledger down

Three lines in a text file next to your output: rows in, each drop with its count and reason, rows kept. Add the file names, the download date and the dictionary date. That is the source, version and modifications the city's Open Data Law asks a republisher to state.

The habit to keep. Read the dictionary, then read the schema, then profile before you aggregate. Every filter prints its count, every drop has a reason, and the ledger travels with the number. A total nobody can reproduce is an opinion.

Check yourself

Three questions

A colleague computes the average tip_amount per pickup zone from the yellow file and maps it as "where riders tip most". What is wrong?

The data dictionary says cash tips are not included in tip_amount or total_amount. A and B change the denominator or the grouping but not whose tips the column holds.

The October 2024 yellow file contains a pickup stamped 2009-01-01. What is the professional move?

The file is complete enough to use and still carries stamps from other months. Keeping them (B) puts a 2009 trip into an October hour-of-week count; discarding everything (C) throws away 3.9 million good rows. Filter, count, record.

13,169 in-month pickups carry zone 1, 264 or 265. What are they?

The lookup's last two rows are "Unknown" and "Outside of NYC", and zone 1 is Newark Airport. A value meaning "missing" inside an id column is still missing.
