Residents who never consented

A customer agreed to terms when they bought. A resident who took a cab or walked past a counter signed nothing with the planning office, yet the rows are about them. Wrenmouth, a constructed transport office in a fictional city, wants to publish what it measures; the counts it would publish come from real New York City taxi records, October 2024. Tonight is how people get singled out without a name, what hiding in a crowd of k buys and where it stops, and why reasonable people disagree about city sensing at all. Nothing here is legal advice.

Part 0

Wrenmouth, on the morning someone asks for the raw data

Wrenmouth is a constructed city transport office. Its counting sensors are being placed (leader session 2), and a local research group has written in: could the office publish what it collects, trip by trip, so they can study travel to work? The analyst from the retail warehouse says yes, after stripping names, plate numbers and card details. In a warehouse that would be the whole job. Tonight is why, for location data, it is the start of the job.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A picture of how a person is singled out from a dataset with no names in it: in a study of 1.5 million people, four where-and-when points were enough for 95 percent. Why making the data coarser buys much less protection than it feels like it should. What k-anonymity promises, in its author's words, and the limit the same paper states. The real cost of hiding small counts in one city's taxi records, which falls almost entirely on the thinnest boroughs. Both sides of the city-sensing argument, stated fairly. And a six-step release review to run before anything about residents leaves the building.

LiveWhy "we removed the names" is not the end4 min▶

A warehouse analyst protects people by removing the columns that name them: customer name, email, card number. What is left, a basket of products, rarely points back to one person, and the customer agreed to be in the table anyway.

A city row is different on both counts. Nobody agreed to be in it. And the columns left after the names go, a place and a time, are themselves the identifier: very few people sleep in your neighbourhood, travel to your workplace and leave at your hour. The pattern of where and when is a fingerprint, and it survives every column you delete except the ones you were asked to publish.

Real world

A New York City taxi trip row carries no passenger name. It carries the taxi zone where "the taximeter was engaged" and the date and time it happened, stamped to the second, plus the drop-off zone and time. Real October 2024 files hold 3,876,702 such pickups in the five boroughs. Whether zone and second are coarse enough to protect anyone is the question Part 1 answers with a mechanism, not a guess.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Spatio-temporal point. One place at one time for one person: a zone and an hour, an antenna and a minute.
  • Unique. Only one person in the data matches a set of points. Unique is not yet named, but anyone who knows those points about someone now has their whole record.
  • Re-identification. Linking a record with no name back to a person, usually by matching it with something already known about them.
  • Resolution. How fine the place and time are. Coarser resolution means bigger zones or longer time slots.
  • Quasi-identifier. A column that is harmless alone and identifying in combination: home zone, age band, shift pattern.
  • k-anonymity. A release property: each person looks like at least k-1 others in the same release.
  • Small-cell suppression. For published counts, withholding any cell with fewer than k events. A related practice, not Sweeney's definition itself.
Part 1 · covers re-identification from location

Four points, and you are alone in the crowd

15 min live

Re-identification is not a hack. It is subtraction. Start with everyone in a release. Each where-and-when point you already know about one person removes everyone who was not there then. Most people share a single point with thousands. Very few share four. In the study the field still cites, four points were enough to leave 95 percent of people with no lookalike at all.

LiveThe mechanism, step by step5 min▶

Start with a crowd. de Montjoye, Hidalgo, Verleysen and Blondel (2013) studied fifteen months of mobility data for 1.5 million people, with location given hourly at the resolution of the carrier's antennas. No names.

Add what an outsider already knows. A neighbour knows roughly where you sleep. A colleague knows where you work and when you arrive. A single receipt or a social post puts you somewhere at a time. Each is one spatio-temporal point.

Subtract. Everyone who was not in that antenna area in that hour drops out. Points multiply each other's effect, because the people who share your night location are mostly not the people who share your lunch location.

Count what is left. The paper's abstract: "four spatio-temporal points are enough to uniquely identify 95% of the individuals." Unique is not the same as named. But once a record is unique, anyone who holds those four points about you can read every other row in it: every trip, every hour, for fifteen months.

Real world

The study used no special access to anyone's identity. It asked a purely statistical question of data with the names already gone: how many points does it take before only one person fits? Four, for 95 percent. That is the number to say out loud whenever someone calls a location release anonymous because it has no name column.

LiveWhy coarser data protects less than it feels like5 min▶

The obvious fix is to blur: bigger zones, longer time slots. It helps, but far less than intuition suggests. The same paper found that uniqueness "decays approximately as the 1/10 power" of the resolution, and drew the conclusion itself: "even coarse datasets provide little anonymity."

Read that as a leader. A one-tenth power is a very flat curve: you have to make the data enormously coarser to make it a little less unique, and by then it may no longer answer the research question it was released for. Blurring is a dial with a poor exchange rate, not a switch that turns identification off.

Apply it to the taxi case without overclaiming. TLC zones are much bigger than a street address, which helps. The trip time is stamped to the second, which does not. Nothing here says anyone has been singled out from those records; the point is that "it is only zones" is not, on its own, an argument a release review should accept.

Self-studyWhat the study does not say3 min▶

The finding is about one kind of data: phone location at antenna resolution, hourly, for fifteen months. Taxi trips are sparser per person, published at a different grain, and most people take few cabs. The mechanism carries over; the exact 95 percent does not, and no page here moves it to a different dataset.

The study also does not say how often anyone actually holds four points about a stranger. That depends on who wants to look and what they already have, which is why the release review in Part 3 asks who could hold the outside knowledge, not only whether the release is unique.

Part 2 · covers k-anonymity and the surveillance trade-off

Hiding in a crowd of k, and where it stops

10 min live

If uniqueness is the problem, the classic answer is to guarantee a crowd. Sweeney's k-anonymity (2002) says a release protects each person if their information "cannot be distinguished from at least k-1 individuals whose information also appears in the release." It is a real protection and a widely used idea. The same paper is plain that it is not the end: it "examines re-identification attacks that can be realized on releases that adhere to k-anonymity unless accompanying policies are respected."

LiveWhat k buys, and what it does not4 min▶

What it buys. Within one release, no combination of the published identifying columns points at fewer than k people. The neighbour with Release 1 alone learns that she is one of three, with three possible stops. That is a real floor, and it is checkable before publishing.

What it does not. The promise is about one release, judged alone. It says nothing about the next release, cut along different columns, or about what an outsider already knows. Put two honest k = 3 releases side by side and the crowds can intersect to one. That is why Sweeney's own abstract ties the protection to "accompanying policies": who gets which releases, and what else is published about the same people.

A sibling session reaches the same verdict from the access-control side: learn data access control, session b5 treats k-anonymity as useful and not a guarantee. We agree.

LiveThe same idea on published counts, and who pays for it3 min▶

A city rarely publishes person rows. It publishes counts: trips per zone per hour. The related practice there is small-cell suppression: withhold any cell with fewer than k events, because a cell of one or two is close to a person. This borrows Sweeney's idea for aggregates; it is not her definition applied to person records.

On real New York City taxi records, October 2024, a threshold of 5 at zone by hour withholds 41.3 percent of the non-empty cells but only 0.7 percent of the trips. The cost is not spread evenly: 100.0 percent of Staten Island's cells go, and 80.0 percent of the Bronx's, against 7.4 percent of Manhattan's. Protection, done honestly, makes the thinnest boroughs vanish from the published picture. Practitioner session 7 lets you move the threshold and the grain yourself.

LiveWhere serious people disagree3 min▶

Should a city measure movement at all, through cameras, plate readers or mobility data for planning? Reasonable people hold both positions, and neither side is short of evidence it finds convincing.

  • For. Planning on counts beats planning on anecdote; services can go where need is measured rather than where complaints are loudest; safety work needs to know where harm happens.
  • Against. People change how they move and gather when they know they are watched, a chilling effect; data collected for one purpose tends to be reused for others, function creep; and the burden falls hardest on people with least say in the decision.

No page here settles it. What a leader can settle is narrower: for this dataset, which purpose, which grain, who receives it, and what would make the office stop. The release review in Part 3 writes those down.

Real world

New York City's Open Data Law, Administrative Code section 23-502(d), says public data sets "shall be made available without any registration requirement, license requirement or restrictions on their use." Once a data set is published, there is no taking back who uses it, or for what. Section 23-501(g) lets agencies hold back what the public officers law allows them to withhold. The decision that protects residents is the one made before publishing.

Self-studyThe law exists, and it is not taught here2 min▶

Data protection law in many places covers location data about identifiable people, and what counts as identifiable is exactly the question Part 1 makes hard. The rules, their scope and their exceptions are taught properly in sibling courses: learn GDPR, session 1 on scope and learn PDPA, session 1. Nothing on this page is legal advice; take a real release decision to the people who give it.

Part 3 · build-along

A release review for Wrenmouth's request

17 min hands on

No code. Take the research group's request to Wrenmouth (constructed): publish trip data so they can study travel to work. Walk it through six steps and end with a one-page decision note. The figure is the trade every step circles: the finer the grain, the more a threshold has to withhold, and the thinnest boroughs lose most at every grain.

Name the people in the rows

Write one sentence: whose movements are these, and did any of them agree to this use? For Wrenmouth's request the answer is every passenger in the month, and none of them agreed to research publication. Keep the sentence at the top of the note.

Ask for the purpose, then the grain it needs

Travel to work is a question about zones and dayparts, not about one rider at 08:14:37. Write the coarsest grain that still answers it. Remember the 1/10 power: coarser helps, but less than it feels like, so grain is a start, not the protection.

Count the points a stranger could match

For person or trip rows, list the where-and-when fields left after the names go. If a regular rider's pattern could be read from four of them, treat the release as identifying. Four points singled out 95 percent in the study that defines the risk.

Set k and say who it erases

For published counts, pick a threshold and then read its cost by borough, not city-wide. On the real October 2024 records, k = 5 at zone by hour withholds 0.7 percent of trips and every one of Staten Island's cells. Decide whether that is acceptable and write down why.

List the other releases about the same people

What else does the office, or the city, already publish about these trips, cut along other columns? Two releases that each meet k can intersect to one. If you cannot list them, the review is not finished.

Write the stop condition and the disagreement

State what would make Wrenmouth withdraw or stop the release, and state both sides of the sensing argument as they apply here, in two lines each. Then send the note to the people who give legal advice. The decision is theirs to check and yours to own.

The habit to keep. When someone says a city dataset is anonymous, ask three things before anything else: how many where-and-when points per person are left, what other releases exist about the same people, and which borough a privacy threshold erases. "We removed the names" answers none of them.

Check yourself

Three questions

A dataset holds months of hourly locations per person, with names and phone numbers removed. Is it anonymous?

The pattern of places and times is the identifier. A treats the name column as the only route back to a person. B helps less than it sounds: the same study found uniqueness falls only as about the 1/10 power of resolution.

An analyst proposes making location data much coarser, bigger zones and longer time slots, and calling it safe. What is the honest response?

de Montjoye and colleagues: "even coarse datasets provide little anonymity." That is a poor exchange rate, not zero effect, so C overreaches. Grain is still worth choosing for the purpose; it just cannot be the whole protection.

Wrenmouth (constructed) publishes two releases about the same residents, and each one meets k = 3. Is each resident protected?

Sweeney's paper examines attacks on k-anonymous releases "unless accompanying policies are respected". Within each release the floor holds, which is why C is wrong; across releases it can fail, which is why A is.

Before session 4

Homework

Twenty-five minutes, on data you could actually be asked to release.

Count the points in one dataset you hold

Pick any dataset about people's movements or visits at work. List the where-and-when fields per person that would survive removing names. Write whether four of them could describe one regular person's week.

Find the second release

For the same dataset, list every other place the same people appear in something your organisation publishes or shares. Mark any pair cut along different columns.

Write both sides in your own words

Two lines for city sensing and two against, about a real proposal near you. Do not pick a side yet; leader session 4 is about reading the claims each side will make.
