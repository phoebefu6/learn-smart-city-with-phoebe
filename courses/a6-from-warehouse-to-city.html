From warehouse to city: the transfer map

Five sessions showed where the warehouse playbook breaks. Part 0

Wrenmouth's analyst, six weeks in

Wrenmouth is a constructed city transport office, and its analyst came from a retail warehouse. Six weeks in, she has stopped asking whether her old skills count. Some count for more than they did: her habit of keeping a ledger of dropped rows caught 47 taxi records stamped outside the month. Some are missing: nobody taught her that a recorded pickup is demand that met a cab. And some are quietly harmful: her first sensor plan covered 88.9 percent of trips and left three boroughs dark. Tonight she writes all three down, and plans the next 90 days.

LiveWhy a transfer map and not a reading list3 min▶

A reading list assumes you start from nothing. Someone arriving from a data team starts from a lot, and the risk is not ignorance, it is confidence in the wrong place. A transfer map sorts what you already know into three piles, so you spend the 90 days on the second and third piles and stop re-learning the first.

The same three columns sit on the landing page under "If you come from a data team". This session is the full version: every item tied to the session that teaches it and to one source you can read for free.

Real world

The yellow taxi data dictionary writes the airport column as airport_fee. The October 2024 file's column is Airport_fee, with a capital A. A warehouse habit, read the schema, not the PDF, catches it in a minute. That is what "carries over" means: a skill that was good practice before and is load-bearing now.

Self-studyHow to read the three columns3 min▶
  • Carries over. Worth more here than in a warehouse, because city data has no author you can ask. Keep doing it, harder.
  • Must learn. Ideas a business data team rarely needs: who writes a row, demand you cannot see, placement under a budget, and people who never agreed to be measured.
  • Must unlearn. Habits that are correct in a warehouse and produce a confident wrong answer in a city. These are the dangerous column, because they feel like competence.
  • The four rows. Reading a row, joining and counting, estimating, publishing. Each column says what that step looks like for you.
Part 1 · covers carries over and must learn

What you bring, and what is new

15 min live

Draw the map once and every later decision gets easier. The left column is your warehouse toolkit, sharpened. The middle column is the city's own grammar, which leader sessions 1 to 3 and practitioner sessions 3, 5, 7 and 10 teach. The right column, green because it is the one that bites, is Part 2.

LiveCarries over: the four habits that matter more here6 min▶

Skill: profile before you aggregate. A monthly file is not a clean month. The October 2024 yellow and green files hold 3,889,918 rows; 47 carry a pickup time outside October, one as early as 2009, and 13,169 pick up outside the five boroughs. A ledger of every dropped row and its reason is what lets anyone trust the 3,876,702 that remain. Practitioner session 1 builds that ledger.

Method: join on the lookup table, and treat "unknown" as missing. The official zone lookup has 265 rows, but two of them mean "Unknown" and "Outside of NYC", and one is Newark airport. Keeping 262 is a decision you write down, not a default. Practitioner session 2.

Denominator: rates that say "of what". The same 40 sensors cover 88.9 percent of trips and 15.3 percent of zones. Both are true. A warehouse analyst who always names the denominator already has the instinct; in a city it decides who gets measured.

Tool class: a reproducible, seeded pipeline with a reference implementation. No product needed: a dataframe library, a fixed random seed, and a second implementation that must agree. Wrenmouth's bench compares the browser engine with a python reference on 3,486 values and finds 0 disagreements. When a budget decision rests on a number, that agreement is the evidence.

LiveMust learn: the city's own grammar6 min▶

Skill: name who writes the row. A meter engaged where a cab was hailed, a sensor where it was bolted, a resident who chose to report. A 2025 study of New York City's 311 heating and hot water complaints found "some subpopulations reporting at lower rates than others" (Boxer, Hong, Kontokosta and Neill). Leader session 1.

Idea: censored demand. A pickup is recorded only where a cab was present to be hailed, so the records measure supply meeting demand. Green cabs may not operate south of West 110th St and East 96th St, by the TLC's own rule. No number for "true demand" exists in the data, and none should be invented. Practitioner session 3.

Method: stratified placement and estimation. Spread a sensor budget across boroughs, estimate per stratum, and report an interval whose coverage you have checked. On Wrenmouth's constructed bench a nominal 90 percent interval covered the city truth about 82 percent of the time over 400 placements, because pickups are so skewed. The honest rung says so. Practitioner sessions 5 and 10.

Tool class: re-identification risk and small-cell suppression. Withhold any published count below k. At k 5 on zone x hour, that withholds 41.3 percent of cells but 0.7 percent of trips, and 100 percent of Staten Island's cells. Leader session 3, practitioner session 7.

Part 2 · covers must unlearn

Four instincts, and the number each one hides

10 min live

The unlearn column is short because each item is a whole habit. Every one of them is correct in a warehouse, where the table is the business. In a city the table is only where the writers were, so each instinct produces a clean, confident number that hides another one.

LiveWhy each instinct fails in a city6 min▶

"No rows means no demand." In a warehouse, no order means no sale. In a city, no pickup can mean no one wanted a cab, or no cab was there, or nobody recorded it. Staten Island wrote 231 pickups in the whole month across 20 zones. That is a fact about the records; what it says about residents is open.

"Measure where the activity is." Wrenmouth's first plan put 40 sensors on the 40 busiest zones: 38 in Manhattan and 2 on the Queens airports. It sees 88.9 percent of trips, and Brooklyn, the Bronx and Staten Island go dark. Its outer-borough estimate is off by +6,388.6 percent. Leader session 2.

"Score the model where you have labels." Compare the busiest plan's estimate only with the zones it measured and the error is 0.0 percent, by construction. Against the whole city it is -11.1 percent, because 222 zones and 430,870 pickups left the denominator. Leader session 4 calls this the break button.

"Aggregated means anonymous." In a 2013 study of fifteen months of phone location data for 1.5 million people, de Montjoye and colleagues found that "four spatio-temporal points are enough to uniquely identify 95% of the individuals." Taxi counts by zone and hour are not phone traces, but a published cell holding one or two trips is far closer to a trace than a city-wide total. That is why practitioner session 7 suppresses small cells.

Real world

New York City's Open Data Law says public data sets "shall be made available without any registration requirement, license requirement or restrictions on their use." The city lets anyone take the rows. The duty to ask who is in them, and what a small cell reveals, travels with the person who publishes the next table.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

Whether k-anonymity style suppression is enough is contested. Sweeney's 2002 paper defines k-anonymity and also examines re-identification attacks on releases that meet it "unless accompanying policies are respected". Some practitioners treat suppression on published counts as adequate for low-detail tables; others argue for stronger formal protection such as differential privacy. Both positions are held by careful people; this page does not settle it.

The same goes for whether more city sensing is good governance or more surveillance. Leader session 3 lays both sides out. What the transfer map adds is narrower: whichever side you take, the warehouse habit of assuming a count is harmless does not carry over.

Write your own three columns

Copy the transfer map and rewrite each cell in your own words, for your own background. An engineer's "carries over" will lead with pipelines; an analyst's with denominators. Cross out anything you already do daily.

Pick one unlearn item to watch for

Choose the instinct you are most likely to fall back on under deadline. Write the number it would hide in your own work, the way 88.9 percent hides 15.3 percent.

Book the first rung

Put days 1-30 in your calendar: read the TLC Trip Record Data page and the data dictionary, then profile one month and write a ledger of every dropped row. Practitioner sessions 1 and 2 are the guided version.

Mark every source

For each item you add beyond this ladder, write official or not, and free, abstract only, or paid. If you cannot tell, it is not official.

Commit to a check at day 90

Write one question you will be able to answer then and cannot answer now. For example: on Wrenmouth's bench, why does a nominal 90 percent interval cover the truth only about 82 percent of the time?

The habit to keep. When a city problem lands, sort your first instinct before you act on it: is this something that carries over, something I must learn, or something I must unlearn? The unlearn column is the one that feels most like experience.

Check yourself

Three questions

An analyst arrives from a retail team. Which of her habits belongs in the "carries over" column?

The ledger is what caught 47 out-of-month rows and 13,169 outside-borough pickups in the October 2024 files. A and B are both in the unlearn column: an empty zone can mean no cab, and the busiest plan leaves three boroughs dark.

Wrenmouth (constructed) scores its busiest-zones estimate against the zones it measured and reports 0.0 percent error. What should a leader say?

Computing error only where you measured is a warehouse habit that hides the unmeasured. The sensors are fine; the denominator shrank. Leader session 4 calls it the break button.

Which source on the 90-day ladder is official?

Official means published by a New York City government source. Open access is about cost, not authority, so A is a free paper, not official. Openshaw is a reported reading: we have not opened it.
