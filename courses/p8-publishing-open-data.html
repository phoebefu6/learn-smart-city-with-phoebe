The chain of promises a city data set passes through, in the publisher's and the law's own words: the TLC did not create the rows and makes no representation as to their accuracy; New York City's Open Data Law puts no registration, licence or use restrictions on public data sets, and lets the city ask a republisher to state the source, the version and the modifications. LiveFree to use is not the same as checked2 min▶

Open data removes the gate in front of a data set: no sign-up, no licence, no rules about use. It removes nothing behind it. The rows still came from somebody's meter, the publisher still makes no accuracy promise, and every change you make on the way to your own release is one more thing a reader cannot see unless you write it down.

Real world

The TLC's trip record page says it plainly: "The trip data was not created by the TLC, and TLC makes no representations as to the accuracy of these data." The rows were "collected and provided to the NYC Taxi and Limousine Commission (TLC) by technology providers". A republisher inherits both sentences, whether or not they repeat them.

Part 1 · covers the TLC page, NYC Admin. Code 23-501 and 23-502, the nyc.gov terms

What the source promises, and what it asks

12 min live

A trip record passes through four hands before it reaches your reader: the provider that sent it, the TLC that published it, the law that governs the publication, and you. At each hand-off the promise either stays the same or shrinks. None of them grows. The figure is the chain, with the one duty that lands on you in green.

LiveThe law's two sentences, read slowly5 min▶

New York City's Open Data Law, Administrative Code section 23-502(d), says public data sets "shall be made available without any registration requirement, license requirement or restrictions on their use." That is the generous half. The same subsection says the department "may require a third party providing to the public any public data set to explicitly identify the source and version of the public data set and a description of any modifications made".

Read as a publisher, that is a three-item checklist with the items named for you: source, version, modifications. It is the minimum a reader needs to rebuild your number from the original, and it costs a paragraph. Whether or not anyone ever asks, writing it is the professional default.

Real world

"Version" is not decoration for these files. The TLC publishes each month with about a two-month delay "to allow time for full vendor submissions" and warns that minor schema changes may come. A count from "the October 2024 file" downloaded on one date and the same file a year later are not guaranteed to agree. The download date is part of the version.

LiveNo accuracy promise, twice over4 min▶

Two sentences frame every reuse. The TLC: the trip data "was not created by the TLC, and TLC makes no representations as to the accuracy of these data." The nyc.gov Terms of Use, section VI: materials are provided "AS IS", without warranties including accuracy, completeness or currency.

Neither says the data is wrong. Together they say the checks are yours, and your reader inherits the same position one step further away. So a derived release carries the no-promise forward in its own words, then adds what you did to earn some trust: the ledger from session 1, with counts.

Self-studyThe hold-back clause, and what it does not say3 min▶

Section 23-501(g) excludes from "public data set" any portion an agency may withhold under the public officers law. In plain words: the law lets agencies hold back before publishing. Pages here make no further claim about when that applies; that is a question for the people who advise on law, not for an analyst.

What it means for practice is timing. Once a data set is out, the no-restriction clause means there is no recalling who uses it or for what. Privacy decisions belong before release: session 7's threshold, session 3's re-identification warning. Data protection law elsewhere exists and differs; for scope, see learn GDPR, session 1 and learn PDPA and DNC, session 1. Nothing here is legal advice.

Part 2 · covers what to release, using the shipped sample

What to release, and what to leave behind

13 min live

The most important publishing decisions are subtractions. The sample file behind every engine page here started as 3,889,918 real taxi rows and shipped as a table of counts. Walk through it as the worked example: what was dropped and why, what was aggregated away, and what the file says about itself.

LiveWhat we dropped, and why it is written down4 min▶

Two drops, both from session 1's ledger. 47 rows carry a pickup stamp outside October 2024, the earliest in 2009: they do not belong to the month the file claims to describe. 13,169 rows pick up in zone 1 (Newark Airport), 264 ("Unknown") or 265 ("Outside of NYC"): none can be placed in the five boroughs, so they leave the 262-zone universe. What remains is 3,876,702 pickups.

Neither drop is controversial. Both are still modifications in the law's sense, and a reader who downloads the October files and counts 3,889,918 rows deserves to know why your total is smaller before they decide you made a mistake.

Real world

The shipped sample carries its own ledger. Open any engine page here (practitioner session 10, say), open the browser console and type CTY_DATA.meta. It prints the source and its web address, the month, the terms, a sentence describing the modifications, and the raw counts: yellow rows, green rows, dropped out of month, dropped outside the five boroughs, kept.

LiveWhat we aggregated away5 min▶

The sample keeps two columns of each row, the pickup time and the pickup zone, and ships counts: pickups per zone per hour of the week (262 zones by 168 hours), the first week's pickups per zone, and yellow and green pickups per zone, plus one centroid per zone for the maps. No row-level trip leaves the build script. Dropoff zones, fares, tips, distances, payment types and exact timestamps are all left behind.

The rule for choosing: release the coarsest grain that still answers the question the release is for. The sensor bench needs counts by zone and hour; it never needs a fare or a dropoff, so they are not in the file. Every column you leave out is a column nobody can misread, join or re-identify.

LiveSuppression: a decision, stated either way4 min▶

Session 7 showed the cost of withholding small cells on these same records: a threshold of 5 at zone by hour withholds 41.3 percent of the non-empty cells but 0.7 percent of the trips, and 100.0 percent of Staten Island's cells. The shipped sample withholds no cells. The reason is specific: the rows it counts are already published, row by row, by the TLC, so withholding our aggregates would hide nothing the source does not already show, while erasing the thinnest borough from every engine page.

Change one fact and the decision flips. If Wrenmouth (constructed) were publishing counts from its own sensors, data nobody else releases, a small cell would be new information about a few people, and session 7's threshold would apply. Either way the publish note says which choice was made, at what grain and threshold, and what it cost by borough.

Self-studyWhen aggregates still single people out3 min▶

Counts are safer than rows, not safe by definition. de Montjoye and colleagues (2013) found that "four spatio-temporal points are enough to uniquely identify 95% of the individuals" in hourly phone location data, and that uniqueness decays only as about the 1/10 power of resolution: coarse data gives "little anonymity". That finding is about person traces, not zone counts, and its 95 percent does not transfer to taxi tables. The mechanism does: a cell of one at a quiet hour is close to a person.

k-anonymity's own author notes attacks that work on releases meeting it "unless accompanying policies are respected" (Sweeney 2002). Leader session 3 walks through how two honest releases can intersect to one person. Differential privacy is the stronger, formal alternative to suppression; it is named here and not taught.

Part 3 · build-along

Write the publish note, and attach it to the file

17 min hands on

Work from the folder and ledger you built in session 1. You will write five fields into a small metadata file that sits next to the data and travels with it. The figure is the note you are building; the green line is the one the law names and the one most often skipped.

Fingerprint the files you started from

A file name is not a version: the same name can hold different bytes after a re-release. A hash is. Run this on each downloaded file and keep the output; it should print one 64-character string per file.

pythonimport hashlib, pathlib def sha256(path): h = hashlib.sha256() with open(path, "rb") as f: for block in iter(lambda: f.read(1 << 20), b""): h.update(block) return h.hexdigest() files = ["yellow_tripdata_2024-10.parquet", "green_tripdata_2024-10.parquet", "taxi_zone_lookup.csv"] hashes = {name: sha256(name) for name in files} print(hashes)

Write source and version

Name the publisher, the page, the month, the date you downloaded and the dictionary date. Put the hashes beside them.

pythonmeta = { "source": "NYC Taxi & Limousine Commission (TLC), Trip Record Data, yellow and green taxi trips", "url": "https://www.nyc.gov/site/tlc/about/tlc-trip-record-data.page", "version": {"month": "2024-10", "downloaded": "YYYY-MM-DD", "dictionary": "Data Dictionary - Yellow Taxi Trip Records, March 18, 2025", "sha256": hashes}, }

Write the modifications from your ledger, not from memory

Use the variables from session 1's build-along so the counts cannot drift from what the code did. You should see 47, 13169 and 3876702.

pythonoutside = ~t.z.isin(five.LocationID) meta["modifications"] = [ {"step": "dropped pickups stamped outside October 2024", "rows": int((~inmonth).sum())}, {"step": "dropped pickup zones 1, 264, 265 (not in the five boroughs)", "rows": int((inmonth & outside).sum())}, {"step": "aggregated to pickup counts per zone and hour of week", "rows_counted": len(kept)}, ] print(meta["modifications"])

State the grain, the suppression decision and the no-promise

Write the grain you released, whether you withheld small cells and why, and the inherited accuracy position in plain words. Then save the note next to the data with the same base name.

pythonimport json meta["grain"] = "pickup counts per taxi zone (262) and hour of week (168); no row-level trips" meta["suppression"] = "none: the source rows are already public; state your threshold and its cost by borough if you set one" meta["accuracy"] = "The TLC makes no representation as to the accuracy of the source data. Neither does this release." pathlib.Path("pickups_2024-10.meta.json").write_text(json.dumps(meta, indent=2))

Run the stranger test

Give the data and the note to someone who was not in the room. Ask them to rebuild one number, say Staten Island's month total, from the original files using only your note. If they get stuck, the note is missing a line. Fix the note, not the stranger.

The habit to keep. No file leaves without its note: source, version, modifications, grain and suppression, no promise. Write the modifications from the code's own counts, hash what you started from, and put the note beside the data with the same name. A release a stranger cannot rebuild is a rumour with a download link.

Check yourself

Three questions

Under NYC Admin. Code 23-502(d), what may the city ask a third party who republishes a public data set to identify?

The same subsection rules out registration, licence requirements and use restrictions (B). Accuracy is promised by nobody in the chain: the TLC makes no representation and the nyc.gov terms say "AS IS" (C). Source, version and modifications are the three items named.

You publish zone counts derived from the TLC's October 2024 files. What should your release say about accuracy?

Aggregating does not remove what a meter got wrong; it adds it up (A). Saying nothing (C) leaves the reader one step further from the source with less information than you had. Carry the no-promise forward and show your ledger.

Wrenmouth (constructed) will publish zone x hour counts from its own sensors, which nobody else releases. On the taxi records, k = 5 at that grain withheld 41.3% of cells, 0.7% of trips and every Staten Island cell. What belongs in the publish note?

Suppression is a modification, and its cost falls unevenly. A reader who does not know Staten Island was withheld will read its absence as zero. A vague line (A) or silence (B) hides exactly the fact that changes the reading.
