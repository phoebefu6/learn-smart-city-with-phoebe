Part 0

Wrenmouth's office asks the wrong question first

Wrenmouth, a constructed city transport office, has tried three rules for placing 40 sensors across 262 real New York City taxi zones (sessions 5 and 10). Its first question was "which rule has the smallest city-wide error?" The better question arrives one meeting later, from someone in an outer borough: "which rule gives my borough a number at all, and how wrong is it?" A city-wide error is an average, and averages are very good at hiding who paid for them.

Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class 🔍 Every diagram zooms

★ What you walk out with tonight. A way to read any placement rule borough by borough: who is measured, who is dark, whose error is large. The denominator problem in two forms: measuring 88.9% of trips while measuring 15.3% of zones, and a "dark zones dropped" score that reads 0.0% when the real error is -11.1%. Why resident-generated data such as 311 complaints carries the same bias. A small pandas scorecard that treats a dark borough as the worst result, never as a missing one. And the surveillance trade-off, held open as a disagreement.

LiveThree rules, one budget, recapped3 min▶

Busiest. Put the 40 sensors in the zones with the most first-week pickups. Spread. Share them across boroughs in proportion to zones by greedy coverage, then draw zones at random within each borough. Equity floor. Every borough gets at least 4, the rest by greedy coverage, with the two airports as a take-all stratum and a busier and quieter half in each borough.

All three rules are constructed: Wrenmouth's budget, Wrenmouth's choices. The pickups they are scored against are real, 3,876,702 of them in the October 2024 files. Every Wrenmouth number below is from the canon run (K 40, seed 1017) that session 10's bench reproduces.

Self-studyWhy "benefit" here means "gets measured well"2 min▶

A counting sensor does not fix a street. What it changes is what the office can see: a borough with a good estimate can argue for buses, crossings or kerb space with a number; a borough with no estimate argues with anecdotes. So "who benefits" in this session is narrow and concrete: whose trips are counted, and whose estimate is close to the truth.

Part 1 · covers per-borough errors and dark boroughs

Read the errors borough by borough

13 min live

Split the same 40 sensors into five boroughs and every rule turns out to serve somebody. The busiest rule serves Manhattan and the two airports, and leaves three boroughs with no number at all. The spread rule measures everyone and misses Queens by -70.7% on this draw. The equity floor measures everyone and keeps every borough within 26% on this draw, while Manhattan does slightly worse than under spread.

LiveDark is not a large error, it is no number5 min▶

The busiest rule puts 38 sensors in Manhattan and 2 in Queens, both on the airport zones, JFK and LaGuardia. Brooklyn, the Bronx and Staten Island get none. Its city-wide error is +482.2% and its outer-borough error is +6,388.6%, because two airports stand in for 193 outer-borough zones.

The three dark boroughs do not appear in that table with a bad score. They do not appear at all. A budget meeting that ranks boroughs by estimated trips will rank three of them with a blank, and a blank sorts wherever the spreadsheet puts it. That is the first rule of reading for equity: count the dark units before you read any error.

Real world

Staten Island has 20 taxi zones and recorded 231 pickups in the whole of October 2024, 0.006% of the month. Under any rule that ranks by recorded trips it is last in line for a sensor. Its residents still travel; the taxi records just say very little about how. A rule that follows recorded trips will keep not measuring them.

LiveOne draw is not a rule4 min▶

The spread and equity rules draw zones at random within each borough, so the grid shows one seeded draw. On this draw the spread rule's worst borough is Queens at -70.7%. Over 400 fresh placements its worst-borough error is 120.5%; the equity rule's is 47.2%. The mean absolute error for the outer boroughs is 92.1% for spread and 6.3% for equity.

So a single run flatters both rules, and the gap between them only becomes clear over many draws. If someone shows you one placement and its per-borough errors, ask for the distribution. Session 10's bench prints it at your own settings.

Self-studyEquity is not free, and the honest rung says so4 min▶

Moving sensors to thin boroughs takes them from somewhere. On this draw Manhattan's error is -23.1% under the equity floor against -19.8% under spread, and the equity rung's 90% intervals cover Brooklyn, Queens, the Bronx and Staten Island but miss Manhattan. Over 400 placements the equity interval covers the city truth about 82% of the time (0.8175) and the outer-borough truth about 77% (0.7675), short of the nominal 90% because pickups are so skewed.

None of that makes the equity floor the wrong choice. It makes it a choice with a cost, paid mostly by the borough that already has the most data. Write the cost down next to the benefit; a plan that claims every borough wins has not been read borough by borough.

Part 2 · covers equity metrics, the denominator problem and unequal reporting

The denominator decides who counts

12 min live

Every equity metric is a fraction, and most arguments about fairness are arguments about the bottom line. Divide by trips and the busiest rule looks superb. Divide by zones and all three rules look identical. Divide by "the zones we measured" and any rule scores perfectly. The fraction you choose is the decision about whose absence counts.

LiveFour fractions, four winners4 min▶

Share of trips measured. Busiest 88.9%, spread 11.8%, equity 20.4%. The denominator is recorded trips, so it rewards measuring where trips are already recorded. This is the anti-lever from session 5 wearing a metric's clothes.

Share of zones measured. 15.3% for all three, because 40 of 262 is 40 of 262. It cannot rank the rules at all.

Boroughs with any sensor. Busiest 2 of 5, spread and equity 5 of 5. Crude, and the first thing to report.

Worst-borough error over many draws. Spread 120.5%, equity 47.2%; busiest has three boroughs with no estimate, which is worse than any number. This is the metric that asks who is served worst, and it is the one the busiest rule cannot game.

LiveResidents' reports have the same denominator4 min▶

Taxi pickups appear only where a cab was present. Complaints appear only where someone chose to report. A map of 311 calls is a map of problems multiplied by the willingness and ability to call, and the two cannot be read apart from the counts alone.

Real world

Boxer, Hong, Kontokosta and Neill (2025) studied heating and hot water complaints in New York City and found "some subpopulations reporting at lower rates than others". Their latent-variable model separates the probability that a problem exists from the probability that it is reported. A study of one US city by Kontokosta and Hong (2021) reports that lower-income and minority neighbourhoods were less likely to report street condition or nuisance issues despite greater need (reported; we did not open the full paper).

So a rule that sends inspectors, or sensors, to where complaints are highest is the busiest rule again, with residents instead of cabs doing the recording. The same reading applies: count the places with few reports before ranking the places with many.

Self-studySurveillance trade-offs: hold the disagreement open4 min▶

Measuring a borough well means measuring its residents more. Reasonable people disagree about where that lands. One position: cameras, plate readers and mobility data give planning and safety value, and leaving a neighbourhood unmeasured leaves it unplanned for. The other: more sensing brings chilling effects and function creep, a counter installed for buses becomes a record used for something else, and thinly measured places may prefer to stay that way. Both are held seriously; this page does not settle them.

Two facts sharpen the argument without resolving it. de Montjoye and colleagues (2013) found "four spatio-temporal points are enough to uniquely identify 95% of the individuals" in hourly mobility data, so finer sensing in a quiet borough means fewer people behind each record. And privacy protection is unequal too: at k 5, zone x hour, small-cell suppression withholds 100.0% of Staten Island's trips, 49.0% of the Bronx's and 0.0% of Manhattan's (session 7). The places that gain most from being measured are the places where publishing is hardest. Leader session 3 holds the consent side of the same disagreement.

Part 3 · build-along

Build an equity scorecard that a dark borough cannot slip past

17 min hands on

You will put Wrenmouth's canon per-borough results into a small pandas table and score the three rules. The trap is in the defaults: pandas skips missing values when it takes a maximum, so a dark borough quietly leaves the metric. The fix is one line, and the habit is the point. The placement numbers are Wrenmouth's constructed canon; the pickup totals are the real October 2024 records.

Write the canon into two tables

One table of sensors, one of single-draw errors, borough by borough. A dark borough's error is missing, so it goes in as NaN, not zero. Each rule's sensors should sum to 40.

pythonimport numpy as np import pandas as pd b = ["Manhattan", "Brooklyn", "Queens", "Bronx", "Staten Island"] sensors = pd.DataFrame({"busiest": [38, 0, 2, 0, 0], "spread": [11, 9, 11, 6, 3], "equity": [11, 9, 10, 6, 4]}, index=b) err = pd.DataFrame({"busiest": [66.8, np.nan, 2724.7, np.nan, np.nan], "spread": [-19.8, -58.4, -70.7, -34.2, -42.3], "equity": [-23.1, 8.2, 25.8, -12.8, -19.9]}, index=b) print(sensors.sum())

Spring the trap on purpose

Take the worst absolute error per rule with the defaults, then count how many boroughs each maximum was taken over. The busiest rule's worst is computed over 2 boroughs, not 5: three residents' boroughs have left the metric without a trace.

pythonprint(err.abs().max()) # busiest 2724.7, spread 70.7, equity 25.8 print(err.abs().count()) # busiest 2, spread 5, equity 5

Make dark the worst result

Any rule with a dark borough gets an infinite worst error, and the dark count is reported beside it. Now no amount of accuracy in Manhattan can buy the busiest rule a better rank.

pythonworst = err.abs().max().where(err.notna().all(), np.inf) dark = (sensors == 0).sum() print(pd.DataFrame({"worst_single_draw": worst, "dark": dark}))

Check the denominator by hand

Under the busiest rule each sensor counts its zone in full, so the estimate of the measured zones is exact. Divide by the measured zones and you get 0.0%; divide by the whole city and you get -11.1%. The working: 3,876,702 minus the 430,870 pickups in the 222 unsensed zones.

pythontruth, unsensed = 3876702, 430870 measured = truth - unsensed # pickups in the 40 measured zones estimate = measured print(round(100 * (estimate - measured) / measured, 1)) # 0.0 print(round(100 * (estimate - truth) / truth, 1)) # -11.1 print(round(100 * measured / truth, 1)) # 88.9

Add the many-draw row and write the paragraph

Your single-draw worst errors are 70.7% and 25.8%; the canon over 400 placements is 120.5% and 47.2%. Put both in the table and label which is which. Then write three sentences, one per rule: who is measured, who is dark, who pays for the improvement. Say that the rules are constructed and the pickups are real.

The habit to keep. Before you report any score for places, write down its denominator and count the units that are not in it. A missing place is the worst result, never a blank that the aggregate skips. Lead with who is served worst, then show the average.

Check yourself

Three questions

A manager says the busiest rule is "clearly the best coverage: it measures 88.9% of trips". What is the strongest reply?

Trip coverage is a fraction whose bottom line favours busy zones. The equity rule measures 20.4% of trips and every borough. At K 80 the busiest rule still leaves 2 boroughs dark (canon), so a bigger budget (C) does not fix the rule.

A report scores the busiest placement against "the zones we have data for" and shows an error of 0.0%. What happened?

Each sensor counts its own zone in full, so scoring it against only those zones reads 0.0% by construction. The number says nothing about the rest of the city.

One neighbourhood files far fewer heating complaints than its neighbours. What can you conclude from the counts alone?

Both A and B read one cause into a count with two. The 311 study found some subpopulations reporting at lower rates, which is exactly why the count alone cannot tell you which.
