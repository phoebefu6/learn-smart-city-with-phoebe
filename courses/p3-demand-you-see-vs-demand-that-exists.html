Part 0

Wrenmouth's planner asks where people want taxis

Wrenmouth is a constructed transport office in a fictional city; it practises on real New York City taxi records, October 2024, because they are public and honestly documented. Its planner asks a fair question: where do people want taxis? The analyst runs a group by and the answer comes back loud: Manhattan holds 88.0% of the month's 3,876,702 recorded pickups on 69 of the 262 zones. Staten Island holds 231. The numbers are right. The question they answer is not the one the planner asked.

★ What you walk out with tonight. Censored demand as a mechanism: a pickup is recorded only where a cab was present to be hailed, so a pickup count measures supply meeting demand, and a zone of unanswered waits reads exactly like a zone of no wishes. One rule you can see in the data: green cabs may not street-hail south of West 110th St and East 96th St, and the yellow share of pickups is 99.0% in Manhattan against 88.5% in Brooklyn. LiveThe warehouse habit, and why it misfires here3 min▶

In a sales warehouse, an order row is the event the business cares about: someone wanted the thing and bought it. A count of orders by region is a fair first look at demand by region, because the shop was open everywhere. A taxi row is not like that. The shop is a moving car, it is open only where it happens to be, and some cars are not allowed to open in some places at all.

Real world

The TLC data dictionary defines PULocationID as the "TLC Taxi Zone in which the taximeter was engaged". The row is written by the meter. A rider who waited and gave up never touched a meter, so there is no column, no null and no zero for them. They are simply not in the file.

Part 1 · covers censored demand, the mechanism

A pickup needs a cab

13 min live

Take two street corners with the same person on each, wanting the same ride. On one corner a cab passes, stops, and a meter is engaged: a row. On the other no cab passes: nothing. The file now says the first corner has demand and the second does not. That is censoring: the count you see is capped by the supply that happened to be there, and the part above the cap leaves no trace.

LiveWhat "censored" means, in one table5 min▶

A measurement is censored when you see it only up to a limit and everything past the limit collapses onto the limit. Here the limit is supply: a zone records at most as many pickups as there were cabs passing and willing to stop. Where supply is plentiful, the count may sit close to the wish. Where supply is thin, the count says "this many cabs met this many riders" and is silent about everyone else.

So read the borough table with two columns in your head that the file does not have. Manhattan: 3,411,386 pickups, 49,440.4 per zone. Brooklyn: 69,631, or 1,141.5 per zone. The Bronx: 13,318, or 309.7 per zone. Staten Island: 231 in the whole month, 11.6 per zone. Each figure is how often supply and demand met there. None of them is how much demand there was, and the gap between them could be large or small: the data cannot tell you which.

Real world

In October 2024, two Manhattan zones and two Staten Island zones recorded zero pickups. A zero in a censored count has at least three readings: nobody wanted a ride, nobody could get one, or the zone is not a place people hail from at all. The homework asks you to look the four zones up. The file alone cannot choose between the readings; only something outside the file can.

LiveThe loop that makes busy zones look busier4 min▶

Drivers go where fares are. Planners place stands, lanes and sensors where trips are recorded. Both are reasonable, and both can feed the next month's counts back into the same places. If supply follows recorded pickups and recorded pickups follow supply, the busy zones stay busy in the data whatever the wish elsewhere was.

Treat that as a hypothesis this file cannot test, not as a finding. What you can say for certain is the direction of the risk: a plan built on recorded pickups alone will tend to reinforce where cabs already go. Practitioner session 5 and the bench in session 10 show Wrenmouth's version of it: placing sensors where trips are already recorded sees 88.9% of trips on 15.3% of zones and leaves three boroughs dark (constructed decision, real counts).

Self-studyThree ways a wish leaves no row4 min▶

No cab came. The rider waited and walked, took a bus, or stayed home. This is the censoring in the figure.

Another service answered. The TLC also publishes for-hire vehicle trip records, the app-dispatched kind. They are not used here, for size and scope, so a ride taken that way is invisible in these files even though it is recorded somewhere.

The wish never formed. People who have learned that cabs do not come to their street stop trying. That demand is suppressed before it can even be censored, and no trip file of any kind will hold it.

Each one pushes the recorded count below the wish, and by different amounts in different places. That is why there is no correction factor to multiply by.

Part 2 · covers the TLC hail rule and the borough table

A rule decides who may stop where

12 min live

Supply is not only a matter of where drivers choose to go. Some of it is written into rules. The TLC's green cabs are street-hail liveries that may not pick up street hails in Manhattan's core, so in that core only yellow cabs can answer a raised hand. The rule shows up in the data as a pattern in which fleet wrote each borough's rows.

LiveThe rule, in the TLC's words4 min▶

The TLC's Green Cab page says street-hail liveries "may not operate in the Hail Exclusionary Zone, south of West 110th St and East 96th St." The page does not say why the rule exists, so neither do we: the rule keeps green cabs out of the core, and that is where the statement stops.

What the rule does to the data is visible without knowing its purpose. In Manhattan, 99.0% of recorded pickups are yellow. In Brooklyn, where green cabs may street-hail, the yellow share falls to 88.5%. Queens is 96.5%, the Bronx 92.9%, Staten Island 98.7% on its 231 pickups. Across the whole month green cabs wrote 56,147 rows and yellow 3,833,771.

Real world

A share like 99.0% is not a fact about riders' taste in cab colour. It is a fact about which fleet was allowed to be there. Read any supply-side breakdown the same way before you read it as preference: first ask who was permitted, present and counted.

LiveTwo airports hiding inside one borough3 min▶

Queens records 382,136 pickups, 9.9% of the city, on 69 zones. Two of those zones are JFK Airport, the single busiest zone in the month at 194,145 pickups, and LaGuardia Airport at 118,732. A per-zone average of 5,538.2 for Queens describes almost no zone in Queens: the airports pull it up, and most neighbourhood zones sit far below it.

Airports are also the clearest case of supply that is organised rather than wandering: cabs queue for arriving passengers. A high count there tells you the queue worked, which is a supply fact as much as a demand one. Session 5 treats the two airport zones as a stratum of their own for exactly this reason.

Self-studyWhy no page here prints a "true demand" number3 min▶

It is tempting to fix the censoring with arithmetic: take Manhattan's pickups per zone as "what demand looks like with plenty of cabs" and scale the other boroughs up to it. That assumes the wish per zone is the same everywhere and only supply differs, which is the very thing you do not know. The result would be a number with the confidence of a count and the evidence of a guess.

Demand that never met a cab is unmeasured in these files, so it is unmeasured on every page that uses them. Estimating it needs a different source: asking people, counting unanswered requests in a system that logs them, or watching kerbs. Name the source you would need, and leave the number blank until you have it.

Resident reports have the same shape. A study of New York City's 311 complaints models the probability of a problem separately from the probability of reporting it, and finds "some subpopulations reporting at lower rates than others" (Boxer et al. 2025). Fewer reports, like fewer pickups, can mean less need or less access.

Part 3 · build-along

Build the borough table, then write the caption it supports

17 min hands on

Same folder as session 1: the October 2024 yellow and green files and the zone lookup, with your script kept somewhere else. You will rebuild the borough table with a service column, check it against the engine on this page, and finish with one sentence. Every number below came from running exactly this code with pandas 2.1.4.

Stack yellow and green with a service column

Read only the two columns you need, tag each row with its fleet, and filter the month exactly as in session 1. The service column is what lets you see the hail rule later.

pythonimport pandas as pd y = pd.read_parquet("yellow_tripdata_2024-10.parquet", columns=["tpep_pickup_datetime", "PULocationID"]) g = pd.read_parquet("green_tripdata_2024-10.parquet", columns=["lpep_pickup_datetime", "PULocationID"]) t = pd.concat([ y.set_axis(["t", "z"], axis=1).assign(svc="yellow"), g.set_axis(["t", "z"], axis=1).assign(svc="green"), ]) t = t[(t.t >= "2024-10-01") & (t.t < "2024-11-01")]

Join the zones and group by borough

An inner join on the 262 borough zones keeps 3876702 rows. The table should show Manhattan 3411386 pickups and a yellow share of 0.990, Brooklyn 69631 and 0.885, Staten Island 231 and 0.987.

pythonzones = pd.read_csv("taxi_zone_lookup.csv") five = zones[zones.Borough.isin(["Manhattan", "Brooklyn", "Queens", "Bronx", "Staten Island"])] kept = t.merge(five[["LocationID", "Borough"]], left_on="z", right_on="LocationID") print(len(kept)) by = kept.groupby("Borough").agg(pickups=("z", "size"), yellow=("svc", lambda s: (s == "yellow").mean())) by["zones"] = five.groupby("Borough").size() by["share"] = by.pickups / by.pickups.sum() by["per_zone"] = by.pickups / by.zones print(by.round(3))

Find the zones the count says little about

Count pickups per zone, including zones with none, then count the thin and the empty ones per borough. Under 100: Manhattan 6, Brooklyn 2, Queens 11, Bronx 8, Staten Island 20. Zero: Manhattan 2, Staten Island 2.

pythonpz = kept.groupby("z").size().reindex(five.LocationID, fill_value=0) boro = five.set_index("LocationID").Borough print((pz < 100).groupby(boro).sum()) print((pz == 0).groupby(boro).sum())

Write the caption

One sentence under the table, naming what it counts: "Recorded taxi pickups (yellow and green), October 2024, by borough: where a cab was present and hailed, not where rides were wanted." Then one line on the hail rule, so nobody reads the yellow share as preference. If the chart goes to Wrenmouth's planner without that sentence, the planner will read demand into it.

The habit to keep. Before you call a count "demand", name what had to be present for the row to exist. If supply had to be there, the count is supply meeting demand, and the caption says so. Never fill the gap with a multiplier; name the source that could fill it.

Check yourself

Three questions

Bronx zones average 309.7 recorded pickups in October 2024; Manhattan zones average 49,440.4. A planner writes "the Bronx has little demand for taxis". What is wrong?

The counts may well be accurate, which is the trap in A: an accurate count of the wrong thing. C confuses a per-zone average with a total. The point is censoring: the row exists only where supply met the wish.

The yellow share of recorded pickups is 99.0% in Manhattan and 88.5% in Brooklyn. What is the best first explanation?

The TLC Green Cab page states the rule. A reads a supply rule as a preference; B invents a defect the data does not show. Ask who was permitted and present before you ask who was preferred.

Wrenmouth's planner (constructed) asks for "the true demand in Staten Island". The file shows 231 pickups for the month. What do you send back?

B assumes the wish per zone is the same everywhere, the very thing you do not know, and dresses a guess as a count. C reads a censored count as a measured absence. Report what the number measures and leave the unknown blank.
