The sensor-placement bench

Wrenmouth, a constructed city transport office, has a budget for 40 counting sensors and 262 zones to cover. Nine sessions took the decision apart. This one runs the whole ladder in your browser on real New York City taxi pickups for October 2024: put sensors where trips already are, spread them across boroughs, weight them for equity with a floor, and then say honestly how uncertain the answer is. Then two traps. One raises the share of trips you see while the estimate for four boroughs falls apart. The other drops the unsensed zones from the denominator and makes the error vanish. The counts are real; the office, its budget and its rules are constructed and labelled so.

Part 0

Where this lands

Wrenmouth's director wants one number for the city and one for the boroughs outside the centre, from 40 sensors. The truth is knowable here, which is the point of a bench: every zone's real October pickups are in the page, so each placement rule can be scored against the full count it is trying to estimate. The office knows only the first week's recorded pickups when it places the sensors; the sensors then count the whole month in their own zones.

Real world

The counts are TLC yellow and green taxi records for October 2024, aggregated to pickups per zone and hour of week. Taxi pickups are not all travel demand (practitioner session 3): the bench asks how well a placement recovers the recorded total, which is a fair test of the placement, not a census of need.

Self-studyWhy the airports are taken whole4 min▶

JFK Airport is the busiest zone in the month (194,145 pickups) and LaGuardia has 118,732. Both sit in Queens among 67 ordinary zones. Draw Queens zones at random and an airport either lands in the sample, and gets multiplied up as if every Queens zone were an airport, or misses, and Queens looks empty. That is why an earlier version of this bench, without a take-all stratum, swung wildly on the outer boroughs.

A zone that is unlike every other zone is measured with certainty, not sampled. That is not the busiest rule sneaking back: it is two sensors on two known giants, chosen because they would wreck the estimate if sampled, with the rest of the budget spread by rule.

Self-studyWhy a 90 percent interval covers 82 percent4 min▶

The interval is the usual stratified one: each stratum's variance, shrunk by the share of it you measured (the finite population correction), added up, and a normal multiplier applied. The normal multiplier assumes the estimate's spread is roughly bell-shaped. Pickups per zone are anything but: in Manhattan a handful of zones carry enormous counts. With 11 sensors, a sample that misses the giants looks tight and low, so the interval is too narrow exactly when it is wrong.

The honest response is not to hide the 81.8 percent; it is to print it beside the 90. The interval arithmetic is the standard one taught in learn statistics, builder session 7; checking its coverage on your own data is the part people skip.

Part 2 · covers the anti-lever and the break button

A lever that backfires, a button that lies

14 min live

A bench that cannot print a worse number measures nothing. This one has two ways to look better while being worse. The anti-lever is the instinct every budget meeting has: put sensors where the activity is. The break button is the instinct every dashboard has: report error only where you can measure it.

LiveThe anti-lever, read honestly5 min▶

Press "Place sensors where trips are already recorded". The bench prints the busiest rule beside the equity rung. Trip coverage rises from 20.4 to 88.9 percent, a gain of 68.5 points that would headline any report. Underneath, sensors outside Manhattan fall from 29 to 2, both on airports, and the outer-borough estimate goes from +22.0 to +6,388.6 percent, because two airports multiplied by 193 zones is not Brooklyn.

The honest nuance: the busiest rule is not useless. If the only question were "how many trips start in the busiest zones", it answers exactly. It fails the question Wrenmouth asked, about the city, and it fails it worst for the places with least data already. Leader session 2 draws why.

LiveThe break button, and why it proves the bench works5 min▶

Press "Drop the unsensed zones from the denominator". The bench compares the busiest rule's measured total with the truth of the measured zones only. Both are 3,445,832, so the error reads 0.0 percent. Nothing was estimated; the question was shrunk to fit the answer. Against the whole city the same total is 11.1 percent short, with 430,870 pickups in 222 zones simply gone.

This is the break button's job: if dropping the unsensed zones did not change the verdict, the bench would not be measuring coverage at all. Because it turns a large error into a perfect score, the bench is measuring exactly the thing a shrunk denominator hides. Any report whose error is computed only where sensors are should be read as this button pressed.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

Should a city place sensors for the best city-wide estimate, or for fairness between neighbourhoods, or where problems are reported? They pull apart. A floor per borough costs Manhattan precision (its error is -23.1 percent under the equity rung against -19.8 under spread on this draw) and buys every borough a usable estimate. A study of one US city's 311 complaints, by Kontokosta and Hong (2021, reported), argues that placing services by reports shifts them away from places that report less; others answer that reports carry real information about need. The bench shows the trade; it does not decide it.

Part 3 · build-along

Write the claim the bench supports

11 min hands on

A placement is a claim about the city: "with these 40 sensors, our estimate of pickups is within so much, everywhere we report it". The figure is the last thing the claim must carry: whether its intervals deliver what they promise.

Run the canon and copy the four rungs

Leave the settings alone and copy the table: zones measured, trips seen, city error, outer error, dark boroughs. Mark which number a slide would most like to quote.

Press both traps and write one line each

"The busiest placement sees 88.9 percent of trips and misses the outer boroughs by +6,388.6 percent." "Reporting error only where sensors are gives 0.0 percent; against the city it is -11.1."

Change the seed, then the budget

Run seeds 2, 7 and 42 and budgets 20 and 80. Note what moves (single-draw errors) and what holds (the order of the rungs, the 400-draw averages). Your numbers will differ from the prose on any setting but the canon.

Write the claim

One paragraph, every number from the bench: the rule, the budget, the city and outer estimates with their intervals, the measured coverage of those intervals, which boroughs are thin, and the trade you chose between city precision and borough fairness. If a number is not printed by the widget, it is not in the claim.

The habit to keep. Never sign off a sensor plan on trip coverage alone. Ask for zone coverage, the dark list, the estimate for the places with least data, the interval and how often that interval has actually been right. And never trust a bench you have not broken: if shrinking the denominator does not change the verdict, the bench was never measuring coverage.

Check yourself

Three questions

Moving Wrenmouth's 40 sensors from the equity rung to the busiest zones raises trip coverage from 20.4 to 88.9 percent. What does the bench say about the move?

Trip coverage is not city coverage. The busiest rule measures where the records already are and extrapolates two airports over 193 outer zones. The city estimates differ too: +482.2 against -17.7 percent, so C is wrong.

A dashboard reports a city-wide counting error of 0.0 percent, computed against the zones that have sensors. What should you ask?

Measuring error only where you measured is the break button. The number is true and answers a smaller question than the one on the slide. B and C both accept the shrunk denominator.

The equity rung's 90 percent interval for the city contains the truth in 81.8 percent of 400 placements. What is the honest report?

An interval is a claim about how often it is right, and this one can be checked. Hiding the check (A) or the interval (B) both remove information. The design matters too: spread's outer interval covers only 29.5 percent.

After the course

Homework

Forty minutes, on your own city or project.

Audit one real placement

Find a published list of where a city's counters, cameras or air monitors are. Count them by district, then write the two coverage numbers you can compute and the one you cannot (coverage of need). Name the districts that are dark.

Rerun the bench on another month

Rebuild the aggregate from another month with the steps in practitioner session 1 and score the four rules. Write down whether the order of the rungs holds. That is the property worth trusting; the single numbers are not.
