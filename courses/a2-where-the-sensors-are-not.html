Where the sensors are not

A sensor plan is judged by a coverage number, and every coverage number hides a choice of denominator. Wrenmouth, a constructed city transport office, has 40 counting sensors to place across 262 zones. The counts it places them on are real New York City taxi records for October 2024, published by the city's Taxi and Limousine Commission. Tonight is what the obvious plan sees, what it cannot see, and why resident complaint data goes dark in the same way.

Part 0

Wrenmouth, the week the sensor budget is approved

Wrenmouth is a constructed city transport office. The director has signed for 40 counting sensors, each able to count every pickup in one zone for a month. The planning team has one week of taxi records to decide where they go, and a slide that already says "maximise coverage". Nobody has asked: coverage of what? By the end of tonight you will ask it first, every time.

Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class 🔍 Every diagram zooms

★ What you walk out with tonight. The difference between coverage of events and coverage of places, on real numbers: 40 sensors on the 40 busiest zones see 88.9 percent of the month's pickups and measure 15.3 percent of the zones. What that plan does to the boroughs it skips: Brooklyn, the Bronx and Staten Island get no sensor at all, and the estimate for the four boroughs outside Manhattan is off by +6,388.6 percent. Why resident reports, such as 311 complaints, go dark by the same mechanism. And a four-row coverage card to hold against any sensing plan before you sign it.

LiveWhy "maximise coverage" is half a sentence3 min▶

In a warehouse, coverage usually means one thing: the share of transactions a table captures. If the table holds 89 percent of orders, it describes 89 percent of the business, because every order is the business.

A city is not its trips. A trip is one event in one place, and the places differ wildly in how many events they produce. So a sensor plan has two coverage numbers, and they can point in opposite directions. Share of events seen rewards putting sensors where events already pile up. Share of places measured rewards spreading them out. A plan that maximises the first can leave most of the city with nothing.

Self-studyThe words that matter tonight3 min▶
  • Busiest rule. Put the K sensors on the K zones with the most first-week pickups. Wrenmouth's first idea; constructed.
  • Equity floor. Give every borough at least 4 sensors, then share the rest by how many zones each borough has. Constructed.
  • Outer boroughs. The four boroughs other than Manhattan: 193 of the 262 zones and 465,316 of the 3,876,702 pickups.
  • Dark borough. A borough with no sensor. Anything said about it is a guess carried over from somewhere else.
  • Expansion estimate. Average what the measured zones counted, then multiply by the number of zones the estimate is for. Simple, and only as good as how typical the measured zones are.
  • Reporting bias. When the chance that a problem gets recorded differs between groups, so the record shows who reports as much as what happened.
Part 1 · covers coverage of trips vs coverage of zones

Two denominators for the same 40 sensors

15 min live

Sort New York City's 262 taxi zones by first-week pickups, busiest first, and walk along the list adding sensors. The share of the month's trips you see climbs steeply, then flattens: most trips sit in a few dozen zones. The share of the city you measure climbs one zone at a time. Those are the two denominators, and the busiest rule spends the whole budget on the steep part of the first curve.

LiveWhy the two numbers come apart5 min▶

Pickups are not spread evenly. In October 2024, Manhattan's 69 zones held 88.0 percent of the 3,876,702 pickups in the five boroughs. JFK Airport alone recorded 194,145; Staten Island's 20 zones recorded 231 between them. The 40 busiest zones hold 88.9 percent of all pickups.

So any plan that ranks zones by recorded trips will show a superb trip coverage almost at once: 61.7 percent with 20 sensors, 88.9 percent with 40, 97.9 percent with 80. Meanwhile it measures 15.3 percent of the zones at 40 sensors. Both numbers are true. Only one of them answers "how much of the city do we know about?"

Real world

The skew is in the published records themselves. The TLC's trip files for October 2024, yellow and green cabs together, put 88.0 percent of the five boroughs' pickups on 69 of 262 zones. Any sensor plan trained on those counts inherits that shape before anyone makes a decision.

LiveThe estimate the busiest rule hands you5 min▶

Sensors exist to estimate what is not measured. The simplest estimate takes the average count of the measured zones and multiplies by the number of zones you want to describe. Under the busiest rule, the measured zones are the busiest zones, so the average is enormous and the estimate inherits it. Wrenmouth's city-wide estimate comes out at 22,570,200 pickups against a true 3,876,702: an error of +482.2 percent.

Outside Manhattan it is worse. The busiest rule puts 38 sensors in Manhattan and 2 in Queens, and those 2 sit on JFK and LaGuardia, the two airport zones. The average of two airports, multiplied across 193 outer zones, gives an outer-borough estimate off by +6,388.6 percent. Nothing is wrong with the sensors; every count they record is right. The placement decided the answer.

Self-studyWhat spreading the sensors costs, and buys4 min▶

Spread the same 40 sensors by how many zones each borough has (11 Manhattan, 9 Brooklyn, 11 Queens, 6 Bronx, 3 Staten Island) and the trip coverage falls to 11.8 percent. The slide gets worse. The estimates get better: -25.6 percent for the city, -67.8 percent for the outer boroughs, and no borough is dark.

Add a floor of 4 per borough and split each borough into busier and quieter zones before drawing (11, 9, 10, 6, 4). Trip coverage is 20.4 percent; the city estimate is off by -17.7 percent and the outer boroughs by +22.0 percent. Neither is perfect on one draw. Over 400 fresh draws the equity rule misses the city by 11.4 percent on average against 21.3 percent for plain spreading. Part 2 · covers dark boroughs and resident reports

Who goes dark, and why the dark stays dark

10 min live

A zone with no sensor is not described as "unknown" on a dashboard. It is described by a number borrowed from somewhere else, or it is left blank and read as quiet. Under the busiest rule three whole boroughs have no sensor at all. And the next budget, built on the counts this one produced, has every reason to put sensors in the same place again.

LiveThe trade, said out loud4 min▶

Move from the equity floor to the busiest rule and trip coverage rises by 68.5 points, from 20.4 to 88.9 percent. In the same move, sensors outside Manhattan fall from 29 to 2, and the outer-borough estimate goes from +22.0 percent to +6,388.6 percent. On the slide that reads as progress. For a planner deciding where buses or kerb space go next, it is worse on every borough but one.

The trap has a loop in it. Sensors placed where trips are recorded produce precise counts there and none elsewhere. The next budget, asked to follow the evidence, follows those counts. A borough that was dark in year one has no data to argue for a sensor in year two.

LiveResident reports go dark the same way4 min▶

A complaint line is a sensor that residents carry. It records a problem only where someone noticed it, knew the line existed, trusted it and had time to call. Boxer, Hong, Kontokosta and Neill (2025) studied New York City's 311 complaints about heating and hot water and found "some subpopulations reporting at lower rates than others". Their model separates the chance that a problem exists from the chance that it is reported.

A study of one US city reports that lower-income and minority neighbourhoods were less likely to report street condition or nuisance issues despite greater need (Kontokosta and Hong, 2021; reported, we could not open the full paper). Neither study gives a number we repeat here. The mechanism is enough: a map of complaints, like a map of sensor counts, is partly a map of where the recorder was.

Real world

The 2025 study did not ask whether 311 data is useful. It asked how to read it honestly, by modelling the reporting step as its own probability. That is the move for any city record: name the step that decides whether a row exists, then ask who that step leaves out.

Self-studyWhere missingness becomes a statistics problem3 min▶

Tonight is about where sensors are placed and who that leaves unmeasured. What happens inside a placed sensor, readings that drop out, drift, gaps that are not random, belongs to a sibling: learn IoT data, leader session 2, missing is never random. The two meet at one idea: a gap in the record is caused by something, and the cause is usually related to the thing you wanted to measure.

Part 3 · build-along

A coverage card for any sensing plan

17 min hands on

No code. Take one sensing plan you have seen or will be asked to fund: traffic counters, air monitors, footfall cameras, a complaint line, a survey. Fill in four rows. The figure shows the card filled for Wrenmouth's two rules; your column starts empty.

Name the event and the place

What does one row count (a trip, a reading, a complaint), and what is the unit of place (a zone, a street segment, a ward)? Write both. Without a unit of place there is no second denominator, which is usually why the slide has only one.

Fill row one: share of events seen

This is the number the plan's champion already has. Copy it as given, and note how it was ranked: if sensors went to the places with the most past events, the number is high by construction, as Wrenmouth's 88.9 percent is.

Fill row two: share of places measured

Count the units with a sensor and divide by all units. If nobody can tell you the count of all units, write that down: it is a finding. Wrenmouth's answer is 40 of 262, 15.3 percent, whichever rule it uses.

Fill row three: who goes dark

List every district, group or time of day with no sensor at all. For a complaint line, list the groups known to report less. For Wrenmouth's busiest rule it is Brooklyn, the Bronx and Staten Island.

Fill row four: error where it matters

Ask which decision the data will feed, and how wrong the estimate is for the places that decision is about. Often nobody knows; write "unknown" and treat it as the biggest risk on the card. Wrenmouth knows only because the bench compares against a month of real counts.

Decide one change

With the card filled, write one change you would ask for before signing: a floor per district, a sensor moved from the busiest place to a dark one, or a second data source for the groups that report less.

The habit to keep. When a plan reports coverage, ask "coverage of what?" and ask for the other denominator. Then ask who has no sensor at all. A plan that sees most of the events and little of the place is measuring where the activity already is, and will keep measuring it there.

Check yourself

Three questions

Wrenmouth (constructed) moves from the equity floor to the busiest rule. Trip coverage rises from 20.4 to 88.9 percent. What happened to its knowledge of the outer boroughs?

Both rules measure 40 of 262 zones, so B looks reasonable, but which zones matters. The busiest rule moves almost every sensor into Manhattan; the two left in Queens sit on the airports. A mistakes trip coverage for city coverage.

Why is the busiest rule's outer-borough estimate so far off, when every count its sensors record is correct?

The error is made by the placement and the estimate, not by the sensors. Nothing in the records suggests airport meters over-count (B), and a single month changes nothing about which zones were picked (C).

A city's 311 complaints about a problem are highest in one district. What does reporting-bias research suggest you check before sending crews there first?

Boxer et al. (2025) separate the chance a problem exists from the chance it is reported. A treats the report as the problem; C throws away useful data instead of reading it honestly.
