Why the warehouse playbook breaks here

A good warehouse team brings habits that work: trust the row, key on the customer, count the sale, refresh the dashboard, fix the refund. Each habit rests on a mechanism, and on city data each mechanism changes. Wrenmouth, a constructed transport office in a fictional city, hired its analyst from a retail warehouse; the rows she works on are real New York City taxi records, October 2024, published by the city's Taxi and Limousine Commission. Tonight is five mechanisms, each drawn as a warehouse row against a city row.

Part 0

Wrenmouth's analyst, three weeks in

Wrenmouth is a constructed city transport office. Its analyst spent years building retail dashboards, and she is good at it. Three weeks in, she has a pickups-by-zone dashboard, a ranked list of the busiest zones and a draft plan to put counting sensors on them. Every step was correct by the warehouse playbook. Tonight walks through why the same steps, on city rows, quietly produce a plan that leaves three boroughs unmeasured, and which mechanism did it each time.

Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class 🔍 Every diagram zooms

★ What you walk out with tonight. Five mechanisms, not slogans: the source (a row someone meant vs a row that exists where a cab happened to be), the identity (a customer who agreed vs a zone and an hour that can still single a person out), the ground truth (a sale is the event; a pickup is supply meeting demand), the feedback loop (a dashboard that refreshes vs sensors that make the counts they read), and the cost of being wrong (a refund vs a borough that goes dark). Each one drawn row against row, plus a five-line audit card to run on any plan before it reaches a budget.

LiveThe playbook is not wrong, its assumptions are local3 min▶

Nothing in the warehouse playbook is a mistake. Joining on a customer key, treating a sale as the fact, refreshing a dashboard daily: each is right because of something true about retail data. A row is written on purpose, by a person the business knows, recording the event the business cares about, in a system whose readings do not change what happens next.

Take any one of those away and the habit built on it starts to mislead. City data takes away all five at once. The method is to name the mechanism under each habit, check whether it still holds, and keep the habit only where it does. Leader session 6 turns the result into a transfer map.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back3 min▶
  • Writer. Whatever creates a row: a meter, a sensor, a resident, a clerk. A city row has a writer that happened to be somewhere.
  • Censored demand. A pickup is recorded only where a cab was there to be hailed, so the record is supply meeting demand, never demand alone.
  • Feedback loop. A measurement that decides where the next measurement goes, so the data starts describing its own placement.
  • Dark borough. A borough with no sensor at all. Anything said about it is a guess.
  • Constructed. Wrenmouth, its budget and its placement rules are invented. The trip counts are real and cited.
Part 1 · covers the source, the identity and the ground truth

Who wrote the row, who it is about, and what it measures

15 min live

The first three mechanisms live inside a single row. In a warehouse, the row was meant, it is about a known customer, and it is the event. In a city, the row exists because a writer was nearby, it is about someone who never signed anything, and it records only the part of the event that met a cab. Each figure below puts the two rows side by side.

LiveMechanism 1 · the source5 min▶

Warehouse. The order row exists because a customer decided to buy, inside a system the business designed. If a row is wrong, there is an owner to ask and a process to fix.

City. A taxi trip row exists because "the taximeter was engaged" in some zone, which needs a cab to be present and a passenger to hail it. The data was "collected and provided" to the TLC by technology providers. Nobody at the publisher meant the row, and the publisher says so. A street where cabs rarely cruise writes few rows, whatever its residents need; the TLC's own green cab rule, which bars green cabs "south of West 110th St and East 96th St", shapes where rows can be written at all.

What breaks. The habit "trust the row, then check it" assumes the row's existence is not itself a finding. In a city it is: where rows exist tells you where writers were.

Real world

The TLC's page states that "the trip data was not created by the TLC, and TLC makes no representations as to the accuracy of these data." The October 2024 files bear it out: 47 rows carry a pickup time outside October 2024, the earliest stamped 2009-01-01. A monthly file from a writer you do not control is not a clean month. Practitioner session 1 opens those rows.

LiveMechanism 2 · the identity5 min▶

Warehouse. The customer key is the centre of the model. The person behind it signed up, accepted terms, and can be asked. Governance is about using a known identity well.

City. A taxi row carries a pickup zone and a time, not a name. That looks safer, and on any single row it is. The trouble is that people move in patterns. In de Montjoye and colleagues' study of fifteen months of location data for 1.5 million people, "four spatio-temporal points are enough to uniquely identify 95% of the individuals", and uniqueness fell only "approximately as the 1/10 power" of the resolution. Coarsening helps far less than it feels like it should.

What breaks. The habit "no customer key, so no personal data" fails. The residents in a city table never agreed to be measured, yet a few of their rows together can single them out. Law exists for this, and it is not taught here: see learn GDPR, scope and learn PDPA.

LiveMechanism 3 · the ground truth5 min▶

Warehouse. A sale is the thing the business wants to count. Model it, forecast it, attribute it: the row and the outcome are the same object.

City. The planning question is "where do people need transport?" A pickup answers a narrower one: "where did a person who wanted a ride meet a cab that was there?" Need with no cab nearby leaves no row. So recorded pickups are supply meeting demand, and true demand is never observed. That is what censored means here. Our data holds no number for true demand, and no honest page invents one.

What breaks. The habit "rank by volume, fund the top" treats recorded volume as need. In a city it partly measures where supply already is. A zone with zero pickups in October 2024, of which Manhattan had 2 and Staten Island 2, may have had no need, no cab, or both.

Self-studyThe same mechanism in resident reports4 min▶

Complaint lines censor in a different way. A problem becomes a row only when someone reports it. Boxer, Hong, Kontokosta and Neill (2025) studied New York City's 311 heating and hot water complaints and found "some subpopulations reporting at lower rates than others"; their model separates the chance of a problem from the chance of reporting it. A study of another US city reports a similar pattern for street condition complaints (Kontokosta and Hong 2021, reported).

Same shape as the taxi case: the row is need meeting a writer, and the writer is unevenly spread.

Part 2 · covers the feedback loop and the cost of being wrong

When the measurement moves the data, and nobody is there to complain

10 min live

The last two mechanisms are about what happens after the analysis. A warehouse dashboard reads the business without changing it, and a wrong call shows up as a refund someone asks for. A sensor plan decides where the next counts can come from, and a wrong plan shows up as silence in the places it skipped.

LiveMechanism 4 · the feedback loop5 min▶

Warehouse. A dashboard refreshes every morning. Looking at it does not change where customers shop. So "follow the data" is safe: the data does not depend on where you looked.

City. Wrenmouth (constructed) knows only the first week's recorded pickups when it places 40 sensors. The busiest rule puts them on the 40 busiest zones: 38 in Manhattan, 2 on the Queens airports. Those sensors count trips in those zones and nowhere else. Next year's plan, if it reads this year's sensor counts, sees the same zones busy again, because those are the only zones anyone counted. The placement has started to describe itself.

One turn of the loop is already expensive. Against the equity rule with a floor of 4 per borough, the busiest rule raises trip coverage by 68.5 points (88.9 percent against 20.4 percent) while outer-borough sensors fall from 29 to 2, and the outer-borough estimate goes from 22.0 percent too high to 6,388.6 percent too high. The bench in practitioner session 10 prints that comparison as "worse".

What breaks. "Follow the data" becomes "follow your own previous placement". The fix is a placement rule chosen before looking at counts, with a floor for every area, which leader session 2 and practitioner session 5 build.

LiveMechanism 5 · the cost of being wrong5 min▶

Warehouse. A bad forecast costs a refund or a write-off. It is priced, it lands on a ledger, and the customer who got the wrong thing tells you. Errors surface on their own, so a team can afford to ship and correct.

City. Under Wrenmouth's busiest rule (constructed office, real October 2024 counts), Brooklyn, the Bronx and Staten Island get no sensor. Their errors are not large; they are undefined. The city-wide estimate is 482.2 percent too high, Queens alone 2,724.7 percent, because two airport sensors stand in for a whole borough. And nobody in a dark borough files a correction, because nothing about them is in the table to correct.

What breaks. "Ship, then fix what customers report" assumes the people hurt by an error can see it and say so. In a city, the people a placement skips are exactly the ones with no row. The error has to be looked for, by design, before the budget is spent.

Real world

Staten Island recorded 231 taxi pickups in the whole of October 2024, across its 20 zones. Any plan that ranks zones by recorded trips will place nothing there. Whether Staten Island residents need transport is a question the taxi table cannot answer, and a plan built on it will never be told it was wrong.

Self-studyHow the fairer rule compares, and what stays open4 min▶

Spreading sensors helps but does not finish the job. Over 400 seeded placements with the same 40 sensors, the worst-borough error averages 120.5 percent under a plain spread rule and 47.2 percent under the equity rule with a floor of 4. Even then, a nominal 90 percent interval covers the true city total about 82 percent of the time (0.8175), because pickups are so skewed. Practitioner session 10 shows every one of these numbers live.

What stays open is whether more city sensing is worth it at all. One side points to planning that rests on counts rather than anecdote; the other to measurement that grows past its first purpose and falls hardest on people with least say. Reasonable people hold both positions. Leader session 3 lays them side by side; no page here settles it.

Part 3 · build-along

Audit one warehouse habit against the five mechanisms

17 min hands on

No code. Pick one habit your team would carry into city work without thinking: a ranked top-N, a daily dashboard, a "fix it when someone complains" process. Run it through the audit card below, one row per mechanism. The left column is what the habit silently assumes; the right column is the question to ask instead.

Write the habit in one line

For example: "fund the ten busiest zones", or "build a daily dashboard of pickups by zone". Write who on your team would propose it and why it worked for them before.

Rows 1 and 2: who wrote it, who is in it

Name the writer of every row the habit uses, and the trigger. Then list what fields describe a place and a time. If four of them could line up for one resident, mark the habit as touching personal data even though there is no name in the table.

Row 3: what the count actually measures

Rewrite the habit's key metric as "X meeting Y". Pickups are need meeting cabs; complaints are problems meeting someone who reports. Write one example of need the metric cannot see.

Row 4: does the habit feed itself?

Ask whether the output decides where the next data comes from. A top-N that places sensors does; a dashboard that only reads does not. If it feeds itself, write the rule you would fix before looking at any counts, such as a floor per borough.

Row 5: who would never report the error

List the places or groups that would have no row if the habit were wrong about them. Under Wrenmouth's busiest rule that list is three boroughs. If your list is empty, look again at row 3.

Keep, change or drop

Mark the habit: keep it as is, keep it with a guard (name the guard), or drop it for city data. Bring the mark to leader session 6, where it goes onto the transfer map.

The habit to keep. Before you reuse a warehouse habit, say the mechanism it rests on out loud. If the mechanism is "someone meant the row", "a customer agreed", "the row is the event", "reading does not move it" or "errors report themselves", check it first. On city data, each of those is usually false.

Check yourself

Three questions

Wrenmouth's analyst ranks zones by recorded pickups and proposes funding new services in the top ten. Which mechanism is she most likely missing?

Recorded pickups are censored demand: need with no cab nearby writes no row. Ranking by them rewards existing supply. A is false, the rows carry a zone and a time, not a name; B borrows the warehouse cost model the session warns against.

Why is a sensor placement plan a feedback loop when a sales dashboard is not?

The loop is about where data can come from, not speed or accuracy. Under the busiest rule (constructed office, real counts) outer-borough sensors fall from 29 to 2 and the outer-borough error rises from +22.0 percent to +6,388.6 percent, and any plan built on those counts inherits the gap.

A city publishes trip counts by zone and hour with no names. A colleague says "no customer key, so no personal data risk." What is the best reply?

de Montjoye and colleagues (2013) found four spatio-temporal points identify 95 percent of individuals, with uniqueness decaying only as the 1/10 power of resolution. C overreaches: publishing with care is possible, and practitioner sessions 7 and 8 show how.

Before session 6

Homework

Twenty five minutes, on habits you already have.

Audit three habits

Run the five-row audit card on three habits from your own team: one about data sources, one about metrics, one about how errors get noticed. Mark each keep, guard or drop.

Find one feedback loop you already run

In any work, not only city data, find one place where a measurement decides where the next measurement is taken. Write what it can no longer see.

Name one dark area

Pick a place or group your organisation's data never reaches. Write how you would know if a decision about them were wrong. If the answer is "we would not", you have found mechanism 5.
