Learn Smart City Data with Phoebe · Leader session 1 of 6

The city as a data source

A warehouse row is a business event someone meant. A city row exists only where a sensor, a vehicle or a form happened to be, and it describes residents who never agreed to be measured. Wrenmouth, a constructed transport office in a fictional city, is the decision-maker for all sixteen sessions; the rows it practises on are real New York City taxi records for October 2024, published by the city's Taxi and Limousine Commission. Tonight is who writes a city row, who is never in one, and how lopsided a real city's records are. Nothing here is investment or procurement advice.

Part 0

Wrenmouth, on the morning the trip files land

Wrenmouth is a constructed city transport office: a director with a sensor budget, a planning team, and a data analyst who came from a retail warehouse. Her first ticket says "show where people need transport". What arrives is not a fact table about people. It is a month of taxi meter records, each written because a cab happened to be hailed somewhere, plus a promise that counting sensors will be bought next quarter. The six leader sessions are about reading that pile as a decision-maker; the ten practitioner sessions are about building on it.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A picture of three ways a city row gets written, a meter engaged, a sensor installed, a resident who chose to report, and of who each one leaves out. The real shape of one city's records: in October 2024, 88.0 percent of New York City's 3,876,702 taxi pickups happened in Manhattan's 69 zones, out of 262. Why Wrenmouth's obvious plan, 40 sensors on the 40 busiest zones, covers 88.9 percent of trips and 15.3 percent of the city. LiveWhy the warehouse instinct misfires here4 min▶

A warehouse analyst trusts three things without naming them. A row has an author who meant it: a customer placed an order. Everyone who matters to the question is in the table: every customer who bought appears at least once. And the table's absences mean something: no order means no sale.

A city row has none of the three. Its author is a meter, a sensor or a resident with a phone, and each writes a row only where it happens to be. The people the question is about, residents who need to get somewhere, appear only when their need met a cab, crossed a sensor or became a complaint. And an absence means nothing on its own: no pickup in a zone may mean no demand, or no cab, or no record. Each gap alone is a caveat; together they change what a count can prove.

Real world

The Taxi and Limousine Commission publishes every yellow and green taxi trip by month. Its own page says the records were "collected and provided" by technology providers, and that "the trip data was not created by the TLC, and TLC makes no representations as to the accuracy of these data." One of the best-known open city datasets starts by telling you who did not write it.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Taxi zone. One of the areas the TLC divides the city into. The official lookup lists 265 rows; the five boroughs hold 262 of them.
  • Pickup. One trip record, placed in the zone where "the taximeter was engaged", in the data dictionary's words.
  • Hour of week. Monday 00:00 to Sunday 23:59 in 168 hourly slots. Most city data is a count per place per time slot.
  • Coverage. The share of something that a measurement reaches. Coverage of trips and coverage of places are different numbers, and the gap between them is the whole of leader session 2.
  • Dark borough. A borough with no sensor at all. Anything said about it is a guess.
  • Censored demand. A pickup is recorded only where a cab was there to be hailed, so the record shows supply meeting demand, never demand alone.
  • Constructed. Wrenmouth, its budget and its rules are invented so the decisions are ours to test. The trip counts are real and cited.
Part 1 · covers who writes a city row

A city row exists where something happened to be

15 min live

The clearest way to see the city as a data problem is to draw who writes each row. In a warehouse, the customer does, on purpose. In a city, three writers dominate: a meter engaged when a cab is hailed, a counting sensor that sees only the spot it was bolted to, and a resident who decided a problem was worth reporting. Each writer is honest about what it saw. Each is silent about everywhere it was not.

LiveThree writers, three blind spots5 min▶

The meter. A taxi trip row begins when "the taximeter was engaged", which happens only where a cab was present and a passenger hailed it. A zone where cabs rarely cruise produces few rows whatever its residents need. Practitioner session 3 turns that into a rule: recorded pickups are demand that met supply.

The sensor. A fixed counter sees its own spot and nothing else. Where it is bolted is a budget decision made before any data exists, and that decision decides which places can ever be described. Leader session 2 and the bench in practitioner session 10 are about that decision.

The resident. A complaint line records people who know it exists, trust it and have time. A 2025 study of New York City's 311 heating and hot water complaints found "some subpopulations reporting at lower rates than others", and built a model to separate the chance of a problem from the chance of reporting it. A map of complaints is partly a map of who complains.

LiveResidents who never agreed to be measured4 min▶

A customer agreed to terms when they bought. A resident walking past a counter, or taking a cab, did not sign anything with the planning office. That changes what the data team owes them. Location records are unusually easy to tie back to a person: in a study of fifteen months of phone location data for one and a half million people, de Montjoye and colleagues (2013) found that "four spatio-temporal points are enough to uniquely identify 95% of the individuals."

Leader session 3 is the whole argument, and practitioner session 7 computes what protecting published counts costs. For tonight, hold one idea: a city dataset is about people who are not in the room.

Self-studyWhat one trip record actually holds4 min▶

The TLC says yellow and green taxi records include pick-up and drop-off dates and times, pick-up and drop-off locations, trip distances, itemized fares, rate types, payment types and driver-reported passenger counts. Locations are taxi zones, not addresses. Tips are recorded for card payments only: the data dictionary says "Cash tips are not included."

So even inside one row, some columns describe everyone and some describe only the people who paid a certain way. Practitioner session 1 opens a real month of these files field by field.

Part 2 · covers where one city's records are

Where the trips are, and where the city is

10 min live

Open the real October 2024 files, yellow and green taxis together, and count pickups by borough. The shape is not a gentle slope. Manhattan holds 69 of the 262 zones and 88.0 percent of the 3,876,702 pickups. Staten Island holds 20 zones and 231 pickups, in the whole month. A dashboard built on these counts would describe one borough in detail and four in rumour.

LiveWhy the records pile up in one borough4 min▶

Yellow cabs cruise where hails are most likely, which is where hails have been most likely before. The city added green cabs to serve the rest, with a rule written on the TLC's own page: they "may not operate in the Hail Exclusionary Zone, south of West 110th St and East 96th St." The rule keeps green cabs out of the core. In our month green cabs wrote 56,147 rows to yellow's 3,833,771.

So the pile of records is a picture of where cabs choose to be, not only of where people want to go. A city that read this table as "where transport is needed" would invest where it already has the most.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

Whether more city sensing makes a city better run, or simply more watched, is an open argument. One side points to planning that finally rests on counts rather than anecdote; the other to measurement that grows past its first purpose and falls hardest on the people with least say. Both describe real outcomes in different places. No page here settles it; leader session 3 lays both positions side by side.

What is not contested is the mechanism on this page: a row exists only where a writer was. Every claim built on city data inherits that.

Part 3 · build-along

Five questions to put to any city dataset

17 min hands on

No code. Take one city dataset you have seen or will be asked to fund, about traffic, complaints, footfall or air, and run it through five questions in order. The figure is Wrenmouth's first idea, and why the order matters: the obvious placement looks excellent on the number people usually ask for.

Name the writer

Who or what created each row: a meter, a sensor, a resident, a clerk? Write it down. If the answer is "a provider we do not control", copy the publisher's own accuracy statement next to it, as the TLC's is above.

Name the trigger

What has to happen for a row to exist? A hail, a vehicle crossing a loop, a complaint filed. Everything that happens without the trigger is invisible. Write one example of a real need the trigger would miss.

Ask for two denominators

A coverage number needs to say coverage of what. Ask for the share of events covered and the share of places covered. Wrenmouth's busiest-zones plan scores 88.9 percent on the first and 15.3 percent on the second.

Ask who goes dark

List the places and groups with no row at all. Under the busiest-zones plan that is three whole boroughs. A blank region on a map is a finding about the measurement, not about the region.

Ask who never agreed

Whose movements or reports are in the data, and what could someone learn about one of them? Note whether the data is published, and at what level of detail. Leader session 3 and practitioner session 7 pick this up.

Commit to a guess about Wrenmouth

With the same 40 sensors spread across the boroughs instead, write down whether you expect the city-wide estimate of pickups to get better or worse, and by roughly how much. Practitioner session 10 checks the guess.

The habit to keep. When someone shows you a city number, do not ask whether it is big. Ask who wrote the rows, what triggers one, coverage of what, who goes dark and who never agreed. A city dataset is a map of where the writers were before it is a map of anything else.

Check yourself

Three questions

Wrenmouth (constructed) places 40 sensors on the 40 busiest zones and reports that its sensors see 88.9 percent of all pickups. What is the right reading?

Coverage of events and coverage of places are different denominators. The busiest zones hold most of the trips, so the first number looks excellent while Brooklyn, the Bronx and Staten Island go dark. C overreaches: the counts are real; the question is what they cover.

A zone records very few taxi pickups in a month. What can a planner conclude?

A pickup is demand that met supply. A and B each read the absence as a fact about residents; the record cannot tell them apart from an absence of cabs. Practitioner session 3 calls this censored demand.

The TLC's trip record page says the data "was not created by the TLC". Why does that matter to a data team?

Provenance tells you where errors come from. The data is official and widely used, so B is wrong; but a monthly file can still carry rows stamped outside the month, which practitioner session 1 finds. That is analytical, not only legal.

Before session 2

Homework

Twenty minutes, on one dataset and on your guess about Wrenmouth.

Run the five questions on one real city dataset

Pick any open dataset from a city portal near you and write one line per question. Do not judge the dataset; mark which questions its documentation leaves unanswered.

List the places your city data never sees

Five of them, in your own words: a street with no counter, a neighbourhood that rarely files complaints, a bus route with no tap-in. Leader session 2 asks what it costs to leave each one dark.
