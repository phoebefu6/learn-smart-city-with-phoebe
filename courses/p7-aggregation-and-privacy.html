Part 0

Wrenmouth wants to publish a count table

Wrenmouth, a constructed city transport office, has a table it is proud of: pickups per taxi zone per hour of the week, built from real New York City taxi records, October 2024. 262 zones, 168 hours of the week, 3,876,702 pickups. Its director asks one question before release: "It is only counts. Is that private enough?" The honest answer is that counts are safer than rows, small counts are the weak spot, and every rule you pick to protect them moves the cost onto somebody.

LiveCounts are safer than rows, not safe by definition3 min▶

A row-level trip file is a pile of points: a zone and a minute where a meter was switched on, another where it was switched off. Anyone who knows where and when you hailed a cab is holding one of those points. A count table melts the points into cells, and a cell of thousands hides everyone in it. A cell of one or two does not hide much: it says that somebody was there, then, and sometimes that is the whole secret.

Real world

The TLC's own files are published as rows, monthly, with pickup and dropoff zones and times. Session 8 covers what a republisher must state; tonight is about the step before that, deciding what grain is safe to put in a table at all.

Self-studyWhat tonight does not cover2 min▶

Access models, masking and tokenisation of person records belong to learn data access control, session b5, which also calls k-anonymity "not a guarantee". Part 1 · covers re-identification from location traces and k-anonymity

Few points, one person

12 min live

Location data has a property warehouse data rarely has: almost everyone's trace is unique. Each point in place and time cuts the crowd you could be hiding in, and it takes very few cuts before the crowd is one. That is the mechanism k-anonymity was invented to resist, and the reason it was never enough on its own.

LiveFour points, and the 1/10 power4 min▶

A study of fifteen months of mobility data for 1.5 million people, with location recorded hourly at the resolution of the carrier's antennas, found that "four spatio-temporal points are enough to uniquely identify 95% of the individuals." Four is not many. A home zone in the evening, a work zone in the morning, one trip you mentioned to a colleague and one photo you posted would do.

The second finding is the one practitioners forget. Uniqueness "decays approximately as the 1/10 power of their resolution. Hence, even coarse datasets provide little anonymity." Halving the precision of places or times does not halve the risk; it barely dents it. Coarsening is still worth doing, it is just not an answer by itself.

Real world

de Montjoye, Hidalgo, Verleysen and Blondel published this in Scientific Reports in 2013. The numbers above are the abstract's, and only the abstract's; the paper is about traces of people, not about any city's taxi release, and nothing here ties it to one.

LiveWhat k-anonymity promises, in Sweeney's words4 min▶

Sweeney's 2002 paper defines it for releases of person records: a release provides k-anonymity if each person's information "cannot be distinguished from at least k-1 individuals whose information also appears in the release." With k 5, whoever you are in the table, at least four other rows look exactly like yours on the fields an attacker could link.

The same paper also "examines re-identification attacks that can be realized on releases that adhere to k-anonymity unless accompanying policies are respected." Read that twice: the definition comes with its own warning. k-anonymity is a property of one table; an attacker holds other tables, and the policies around the release are part of the protection.

Self-studyFrom person records to published counts4 min▶

Wrenmouth is not releasing person records; it is releasing counts. Tonight's rule borrows the idea, not the definition: a cell with fewer than k pickups is withheld. That is small-cell suppression, a related practice for published counts. It is not Sweeney's k-anonymity applied to rows, and calling it k-anonymity in a release note overstates what it does.

The logic transfers well enough to be useful. A cell of 1 says one trip started in that zone in that hour of the week across the month; anyone who knows they were there now knows it was them, and anyone who knows a neighbour took a cab then has a strong hint. A cell of 300 says much less about any one rider. Choosing k is choosing the smallest crowd you are willing to publish.

Whether that is enough is a genuine disagreement. One position: for aggregate tables of a busy service, suppression with sensible policies is proportionate and keeps the data useful. The other: only a formal guarantee holds up against attackers with other data, so suppression is a habit, not a protection. Reasonable people hold both. Your job tonight is to know exactly what your rule withholds and who pays for it.

Part 2 · covers small-cell suppression on zone x hour cells and the areal unit problem

Small cells, thin boroughs

13 min live

Take every October pickup in the five boroughs, count them per zone per hour of the week, and withhold every cell under k. City-wide, almost nothing is lost. Borough by borough, the picture inverts: the places with the fewest recorded trips are exactly the places whose cells are smallest, so they are the places the release goes quiet about.

LiveReading the grid: cheap for the city, dear for the thin places5 min▶

At k 5 on zone x hour, 12,772 of 30,898 non-empty cells are withheld: 41.3% of cells, but only 0.7% of trips, because the withheld cells are small by definition. Raise k to 10 and it becomes 55.4% of cells and 1.4% of trips; lower it to 3 and it is 28.1% and 0.3%. On a city-wide total, the rule is almost invisible.

Now read it by borough. Manhattan loses 7.4% of its cells and 0.0% of its trips. Brooklyn loses 52.1% of cells and 12.9% of trips; Queens 53.3% and 2.3%; the Bronx 80.0% and 49.0%. Staten Island loses 100.0% of its cells and 100.0% of its trips: at this grain, the release says nothing at all about it.

Real world

Staten Island recorded 231 pickups in the whole of October 2024 across its 20 zones. Spread over 168 hours of the week, no cell reaches 5. The privacy rule did not choose to erase a borough; the thinness of the recorded data did, and the rule made it final. Practitioner session 4 showed where the data is thin; this is what thin data does to a release.

LiveGrain is a choice: the areal unit problem5 min▶

Openshaw's 1984 monograph on the modifiable areal unit problem is reported here, not quoted: results computed on areal units change with the size and the boundaries of the units. You do not need his numbers to see it; ours show it. Same trips, same k 5, four groupings, four different stories about how much is withheld and from whom.

Self-studyWithheld is not hidden: subtraction, and the stronger alternative4 min▶

Suppression has a classic leak. If Wrenmouth publishes each zone's monthly total alongside the zone x hour cells, a zone with exactly one withheld cell gives it away: total minus the published cells equals the withheld count. That is one of the "accompanying policies" Sweeney's warning is about. The fix is a rule, not cleverness: either withhold a second cell in that row so subtraction cannot isolate one, or do not publish totals that let it.

Differential privacy is the stronger alternative: it adds calibrated noise so that any one person's presence changes the published numbers only within a stated bound. It is named here and not taught; it trades exact counts for a guarantee, and the same disagreement applies about whether a city release needs it.

For checking intervals and uncertainty around noisy or small counts, learn statistics, session b7 is the sibling to read.

Part 3 · build-along

Build the cells, suppress them, write the release note

17 min hands on

Start from the kept frame and the five zone table from practitioner session 1: 3,876,702 in-month pickups in the 262 zones of the five boroughs, with a pickup time t and a zone z. The engine on this page built its cells from the same rows with the same hour-of-week rule, so your counts should match the canon to the cell. The figure is the ledger you are building.

Build zone x hour-of-week cells

Hour of the week is the day (Monday 0) times 24 plus the hour. Grouping keeps only cells with at least one pickup, which is what "non-empty" means. You should see 30898.

pythonkept = kept.assign(how=kept.t.dt.dayofweek * 24 + kept.t.dt.hour) cells = kept.groupby(["z", "how"]).size() print(len(cells), cells.sum())

Withhold every cell under k, and print what it cost

Three numbers: cells withheld, share of cells, share of trips. At k 5 you should see 12772, 41.3 and 0.7. Try k 3 and k 10 and check them against the widget.

pythonk = 5 small = cells < k print(small.sum(), round(small.mean() * 100, 1), round(cells[small].sum() / cells.sum() * 100, 1))

Split the cost by borough

This is the line most releases skip. You should see Manhattan 7.4, Brooklyn 52.1, Queens 53.3, Bronx 80.0 and Staten Island 100.0 for cells, and 0.0, 12.9, 2.3, 49.0 and 100.0 for trips.

pythonboro = five.set_index("LocationID").Borough b = cells.rename("n").reset_index().assign(B=lambda d: d.z.map(boro)) b["held"] = b.n < k print(b.groupby("B").held.mean().mul(100).round(1)) print(b[b.held].groupby("B").n.sum().div(b.groupby("B").n.sum()).mul(100).round(1))

Change the grain and watch the story move

Daypart is the day times 4 plus the hour floor-divided by 6. At k 5 you should see 6498 cells with 18.4% withheld. Then group by borough instead of zone: 778 cells, 13.0% withheld, and Staten Island still 95.3%.

pythonkept = kept.assign(dp=kept.t.dt.dayofweek * 4 + kept.t.dt.hour // 6, B=kept.z.map(boro)) for keys in (["z", "dp"], ["B", "how"]): c = kept.groupby(keys).size() print(keys, len(c), round((c < k).mean() * 100, 1))

Publish the cells and write the release note

Keep the published cells only, and write a note beside the file: the source and month, k, the grain, cells and trips withheld city-wide and per borough, and whether any totals are also published (if they are, check that no row has exactly one withheld cell). Session 8 turns this note into a full publishing checklist.

pythonout = b[~b.held][["z", "how", "n"]] out.to_csv("wrenmouth_pickups_zone_hour_k5.csv", index=False) print(len(out))

The habit to keep. Never report a suppression rule by its city-wide cost alone. Print what it withholds borough by borough, choose the grain on purpose, and ship the note with the table. "0.7% of trips withheld" and "Staten Island withheld entirely" are the same release; a reader deserves both sentences.

Check yourself

Three questions

At k 5 on zone x hour, Wrenmouth's release withholds 41.3% of cells but only 0.7% of trips. A manager says the rule is "basically free". What is the best reply?

Withheld cells are small by definition, so they carry few trips city-wide; they are concentrated where recorded trips are thin. Manhattan loses 7.4% of cells and 0.0% of trips. Report the cost by borough.

A colleague writes: "We applied k-anonymity with k 5 to the count table, so the release is anonymous." What is wrong with the sentence?

Neither suppression nor k-anonymity is a guarantee on its own. Raising k (C) withholds more (55.4% of cells at k 10) without changing what kind of protection it is.

Moving from zone x hour to borough x hour at k 5 drops withheld cells from 41.3% to 13.0%. What happened?

This is the areal unit problem with our own numbers: results change with the size and boundaries of the units. Same rows, same k, a different story.

Cheat sheet

Four points. Enough to single out 95% of people in a mobility trace (2013 study).
Coarsening. Uniqueness falls only as the 1/10 power of resolution. Helps little alone.
k-anonymity. Each person indistinguishable from k-1 others. Not a guarantee by itself.
Suppression. Withhold every count under k. A related practice for counts, not the definition.
The canon. k 5, zone x hour: 41.3% of cells, 0.7% of trips withheld.
Who pays. Bronx 80.0% of cells; Staten Island 100.0%. Manhattan 7.4%.
Grain. Same trips, bigger units, fewer withheld cells. The story moves with the unit.
Release note. k, grain, what was withheld, per borough. No totals that leak by subtraction.
