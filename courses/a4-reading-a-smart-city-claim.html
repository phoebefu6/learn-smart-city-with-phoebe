Reading a smart-city claim

"The pilot cut delays." "Our sensors count the city with no error." "The charge worked." Every one of these arrives as a number with a story attached, and the story is where the decision gets made. Wrenmouth, a constructed transport office in a fictional city, receives three such claims tonight; the numbers behind them come from real New York City taxi records, October 2024, published by the city's Taxi and Limousine Commission. Tonight is five questions that sit between a claim and a budget, and two mechanisms that make bad claims look good.

Part 0

Wrenmouth, on the morning three claims land

Wrenmouth is a constructed city transport office. On one morning its director gets three memos. The roads team says new signage at the five worst junctions cut delays the following month. The sensor team says its counters measure the city's taxi trips with an error of 0.0 percent. And a council member forwards two evaluations of another city's congestion charge that reach opposite conclusions. Each memo asks for money or a signature. None is lying. Two of them are wrong in ways the memo cannot show, and the third is a disagreement nobody can settle by reading harder.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A five-question checklist for any smart-city claim: compared with what, how were the places chosen, who is in the denominator, what else changed, and how sure are we about whom. Why picking the worst junctions and measuring them again shows an improvement even when nothing was done. How dropping the 222 zones nobody measured turns a real error of -11.1 percent into a reported 0.0 percent, on real October 2024 taxi counts. And a way to hold a contested question, congestion pricing, without pretending it is settled.

LiveWhat counts as a smart-city claim3 min▶

A claim is any sentence that ties a city number to a cause or a quality: this intervention changed that outcome, this measurement is accurate, this place needs more. In a warehouse a claim can usually be checked against the ledger, because the ledger is the business. In a city the rows exist only where a meter, a sensor or a resident happened to be, so the check itself is built from partial rows. A claim is not wrong for being partial; it is wrong when it hides which part.

Tonight's habit is to treat every claim as a question the author should already have answered, and to ask for the answer before the money moves.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Before-after claim. "We measured, we acted, we measured again, it got better." The weakest design a city uses, and the most common.
  • Regression to the mean. Unusually high or low readings tend to be followed by readings closer to the average, with no cause at all.
  • Selected on the baseline. Places chosen because their first reading was extreme. The design that makes regression to the mean biggest.
  • Denominator. What a share or an error is divided by. Change it quietly and any number can look good.
  • Comparison group. Places that did not get the treatment, measured the same way at the same time. The first thing a before-after claim lacks.
  • Interval. A range that should contain the truth a stated share of the time. Whether it really does is a separate question.
  • Constructed. Wrenmouth, its memos and its junctions are invented. The taxi counts and the bench results are real and cited.
Part 1 · covers regression to the mean in before-after claims

Pick the worst, measure again, watch them improve

15 min live

The roads team did something every city does. It ranked junctions by last month's delays, picked the five worst, put up signage, and counted again a month later. The five were better. The problem is not the signage; it is the picking. Any count mixes a lasting level with a month of luck, and the five that came top of the list were, on average, the ones that also had the worst luck. Luck does not repeat on schedule, so the next count drifts back towards the middle whether or not anyone did anything.

LiveWhy the worst get better on their own6 min▶

Think of each junction's monthly delay as two parts: a lasting level (the road layout, the traffic it carries) and a month of noise (a burst main, a festival, a wet week). Rank all junctions by one month's count and the top five are the ones where both parts were high. Next month the level is the same and the noise is fresh, so on average the five come back down. A team that intervened in between sees a fall and, reasonably, credits the intervention.

Nothing in the memo is false. The count really fell. What the memo cannot show is how much of the fall would have happened anyway, because it has no junctions that were picked the same way and left alone.

Real world

Barnett, van der Pols and Dobson (2005), writing for epidemiologists, put it plainly: regression to the mean "can make natural variation in repeated data look like real change", and "should always be considered as a possible cause of an observed change." They add that the effect grows with measurement error and when follow-up is examined only on a sub-sample selected using a baseline value. Picking the five worst junctions is exactly that sub-sample.

LiveWhen the effect is biggest4 min▶

Two conditions from the same paper turn regression to the mean from a footnote into the whole result. First, noisy counts: the more a single month swings, the more of the "worst" ranking is luck. Thin city data is noisy by nature; in our October 2024 taxi month, Staten Island's 20 zones recorded 231 pickups in total, and a handful of trips either way would reorder any ranking of them. Second, selection on the baseline: places chosen because they looked extreme, which is how pilots and hot-spot programmes are usually chosen.

So the questions for the roads team are not "did it fall" but "how were the five picked" and "what happened to the junctions you did not touch".

Self-studyWhat a better design looks like5 min▶

Barnett and colleagues say regression to the mean is dealt with by design and suitable methods. The plain versions a leader can ask for:

  • A comparison group. Rank the junctions, then treat some of the worst and leave comparable ones alone, chosen by a seeded random draw rather than by judgement. Both groups regress; only the difference is the effect.
  • Select on one count, judge on another. Pick the places using an earlier period, then compare later periods, so the luck that got them picked is not in the before figure.
  • More than one before reading. A year of counts shows the lasting level far better than one bad month.

Estimating the effect properly, with difference-in-differences or a synthetic control, is not taught here. It is taught in learn experimentation, difference-in-differences and geo tests and synthetic control. Practitioner session 6 frames the same before-after problem for the people who build the counts.

Part 2 · covers the denominator and contested claims

An error of 0.0 percent, and the zones it left out

10 min live

The sensor team's memo is the one that should worry a director most, because its number is correct. Wrenmouth placed 40 counters on the 40 busiest of New York City's 262 taxi zones, using real October 2024 counts. Each counter counts every pickup in its zone for the month. Checked against the true pickups of those 40 zones, the error is 0.0 percent, by construction. Checked against the whole city, the same count misses 11.1 percent of pickups. Nothing was measured badly; the city was quietly made smaller.

LiveThe 0.0 percent that is true and useless4 min▶

Any accuracy figure is a comparison: estimate against truth. The trick is in choosing which truth. Compare the counters only with the zones they sit in, and they cannot be wrong, because they are the truth for those zones. The bench in practitioner session 10 keeps this as a "break button": press it and the reported error falls to 0.0 percent while the real error against the city stays at -11.1 percent, because 222 zones and 430,870 pickups have left the denominator.

The tell is in the wording. "Error against measured locations", "accuracy where data is available", "for zones with coverage": each of these is a 0.0 percent waiting to happen. Ask for the error against the place the decision is about. In leader session 2 the same placement looked excellent on another number, 88.9 percent of trips covered, while 15.3 percent of zones were measured and three boroughs had no counter at all.

LiveCongestion pricing: where serious people disagree4 min▶

The council member's two evaluations are a different kind of problem. Evaluations of congestion pricing and road-use charges disagree about how large the effect is, how long it lasts and who bears the cost. Some of that disagreement is about design: a before-after comparison of a charged zone is exposed to regression to the mean, to seasons, and to everything else that changed in the same months. Some of it is about values: whose journeys count, and whether a faster core is worth a dearer trip for people who cannot avoid it.

No figure from any city's scheme appears on these pages, and none is needed to do the useful thing: run each evaluation through the same five questions, write down where they differ, and accept that a leader may have to decide while the question is still open. Treating the more recent or the more confident study as the winner is not reading; it is choosing.

Real world

The only fact these pages state about New York's own scheme is a schema change. The TLC's yellow taxi data dictionary (March 18, 2025) adds a column, cbd_congestion_fee, described as a "Per-trip charge for MTA's Congestion Relief Zone starting Jan. 5, 2025." Our October 2024 file predates it, so the column is absent. Any before-after study that spans that date is also comparing two different schemas.

Self-studyHow sure is "how sure"4 min▶

Even an honest claim with a range attached has a second claim inside it: that the range means what it says. On the bench, Wrenmouth's equity placement reports a nominal 90 percent interval for city pickups. Over 400 fresh placements that interval covers the truth 81.75 percent of the time, about 82 percent, because pickups are so skewed across zones. On the canon draw itself, the interval of 2,744,659 to 3,638,364 misses the true 3,876,702.

The question for any memo with a range is: "how often has a range built this way contained the truth?" Most memos cannot answer it. The ones that can are worth more. For how intervals are built, see learn statistics, confidence intervals.

Part 3 · build-along

Five questions between a claim and a decision

17 min hands on

No code. Take one claim you have seen in a memo, a press release or a briefing, about delays, footfall, complaints or air, and walk it through five gates in order. The figure walks Wrenmouth's signage claim (constructed) through the same gates. A claim that clears all five is a finding worth acting on; a claim that stalls at one goes back with that question attached.

Checklist Tick a gate only when the claim's author has answered it, not when you can guess the answer. Nothing is saved or sent.

0 of 5 answered: still a claim.

Static checklist. Your ticks stay in this tab only.

Write the claim as one sentence

Strip it to "X caused Y" or "X is accurate to Z". If you cannot, the memo is not making a claim yet; send it back for one.

Ask what it is compared with

Look for places or periods that did not get the treatment. If the only comparison is the same places before, write "before-after only" next to the claim.

Ask how the places were picked

Worst, busiest, most complained-about: any choice made on an extreme reading predicts improvement with no treatment. Note which it was.

Find the denominator

For every share or error, write what it is divided by. If the answer is "the places we measured", ask for the number against the whole area. Wrenmouth's 0.0 percent becomes -11.1 percent.

List what else changed

Season, a new fare, a new fee, a road closure, a change in how the data is recorded. Each is a rival explanation the claim must rule out.

Write back, do not rule

Return the claim with the gates it has not cleared and the cheapest design that would clear them. For a formal audit of causal claims, use learn causal inference, the claim audit.

The habit to keep. When a city number arrives with a cause attached, do not ask whether you believe it. Ask compared with what, how the places were chosen, divided by what, what else changed, and how sure about whom. A claim that answers all five is a finding. A claim that cannot is a hypothesis with a budget request stapled to it.

Check yourself

Three questions

Wrenmouth (constructed) picks the five junctions with the worst delays last month, adds signage, and next month delays at those five are lower. What is the right reading?

Junctions selected for an extreme baseline drift back towards the mean on their own (Barnett et al., 2005). The fall may be partly real, so A overclaims; nothing suggests the counts are wrong, so B dismisses the data for the wrong reason.

A memo says Wrenmouth's 40 counters measure taxi pickups with an error of 0.0 percent. What do you ask first?

The denominator decides the error. Dropping the 222 unsensed zones and their 430,870 pickups makes any count look exact. B accepts the number without its denominator; C misreads what an error of zero means.

Two evaluations of a congestion charge reach different conclusions about its effect. What is the sound next step?

Evaluations disagree on size, durability and who bears the cost, and before-after designs are exposed to regression to the mean, seasons and concurrent changes. Recency (A) is not a design, and averaging (C) blends a good design with a weak one.

Before session 5

Homework

Twenty minutes, on one real claim and one of your own.

Run one public city claim through the five gates

Find a claim in a city press release or annual report: a pilot that cut something, a scheme that improved something. Write one line per gate and mark which gates the source answers. Do not decide whether it is true.

Find one denominator in your own work

Pick a share or error your team reports and write down exactly what it is divided by. If any place or group drops out of that denominator, write the sentence your report would need to say so.

Redesign one before-after

Take a before-after you have seen and sketch, in three lines, the comparison group and the selection rule that would have made it a finding.
