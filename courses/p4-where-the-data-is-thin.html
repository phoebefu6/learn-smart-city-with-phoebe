Where the data is thin

A city dataset is thick where the measuring happens and thin everywhere else. Tonight you find the thin places in real New York City taxi records, October 2024: zones with fewer than 100 pickups in a month, a whole borough with 231. Wrenmouth, a constructed city transport office, is about to place sensors using counts like these, so before session 5 it needs to know which counts can carry a decision and which are mostly noise.

LiveThin is a property of the measurement3 min▶

A taxi pickup is recorded only where a cab was present to be hailed and switched its meter on. So a zone with few pickups is a zone where few meters were switched on. That might be a quiet place. It might be a place cabs rarely pass. The row count cannot say which, and every number on this page is a count of meters, not of people.

Real world

Staten Island holds 20 of New York City's 262 taxi zones, 7.6% of them. In October 2024 its zones recorded 231 yellow and green pickups between them, a 0.006% share of the month. Manhattan, with 69 zones, recorded 3,411,386. Both numbers come from the same files, the same meters and the same filter. One of them can carry a decision about a single zone; the other cannot.

Self-studyWhere tonight's numbers come from2 min▶

Every count below is derived from the TLC's October 2024 yellow and green trip records after session 1's ledger: 47 out-of-month rows and 13,169 pickups outside the five boroughs dropped, 3,876,702 kept. Zones and boroughs come from the TLC Taxi Zone Lookup, joined zones-first as in session 2 so that a zone with no pickups is still a row.

The TLC says the data "was not created by the TLC, and TLC makes no representations as to the accuracy of these data." A thin count inherits that sentence with less room for error: one mis-stamped row is invisible among 49,440 and visible among 11.

Part 1 · covers pickups per zone by borough

Where the counts run out

12 min live

Manhattan and Queens have the same number of zones and differ in pickups per zone by a factor of nine; Manhattan and Staten Island differ by a factor of more than four thousand. Draw each borough as its zones, colour the ones under 100 pickups for the month, and the thin data stops being a footnote and becomes a shape: a small slice of Manhattan, a large slice of Queens and the Bronx, all of Staten Island.

LiveSame number of zones, very different counts4 min▶

Manhattan and Queens each have 69 zones. Manhattan's average 49,440.4 pickups per zone; Queens' average 5,538.2, and Queens includes JFK and LaGuardia, the first and one of the busiest zones in the whole month. Take the airports out of your head and the rest of Queens is thinner than its average suggests. Brooklyn's 61 zones average 1,141.5, the Bronx's 43 zones 309.7, Staten Island's 20 zones 11.6.

Count zones under 100 pickups for the month and the pattern sharpens: Manhattan 6 of 69, Brooklyn 2 of 61, Queens 11 of 69, Bronx 8 of 43, Staten Island 20 of 20. That is 47 of the 262 zones (a different 47 from session 1's out-of-month rows: a coincidence of the arithmetic, nothing more).

Real world

The outer boroughs, everything but Manhattan, hold 193 of the 262 zones and 465,316 of the 3,876,702 pickups. Manhattan holds 88.0% of the month's pickups on 69 zones. Any per-zone statistic computed on this file is precise in a quarter of the city and fragile in most of the rest.

LiveZero is a row too4 min▶

Four zones recorded no pickup at all in October: two in Manhattan, two in Staten Island. A groupby on the pickup zone never shows them, because a group needs at least one row to exist. The zone table quietly shrinks from 262 to 258, and every average, rank and map built on it is about 258 zones while its title says the city.

Session 2's zones-first join is the cure: start from the 262 zones, attach counts, fill the gaps with 0. A zero you can see is a finding. A zero that fell out of the table is a hole in the denominator nobody will ever notice.

Self-studyFew events or few observers: the file cannot tell4 min▶

A thin cell has two possible causes. Few events: few people wanted a cab there. Few observers: people wanted one, but no cab was there to be hailed, so no meter switched on and no row was written. The TLC's own rules show the second cause is real: green cabs "may not operate in the Hail Exclusionary Zone, south of West 110th St and East 96th St", so the rule keeps green cabs out of the core and the two fleets record different parts of the city. Session 3 teaches this as censored demand; tonight it is enough that thin pickups are not proof of thin need.

Real world

Resident reports have the same shape. Boxer, Hong, Kontokosta and Neill (2025) model New York City 311 complaints about heating and hot water and find "some subpopulations reporting at lower rates than others"; their model separates the probability of a problem from the probability of reporting it. A study of one US city's 311 data reports that lower-income and minority neighbourhoods were less likely to report street problems despite greater need (Kontokosta and Hong, 2021, reported). In both, a thin cell is partly a thin observer.

Part 2 · covers why a thin cell is noise

A thin cell is noise, not a finding

13 min live

Every slice you add to a table divides the same rows into more cells. A borough becomes zones, a zone becomes hours of the week, and somewhere along the way the counts in each cell become so small that the next month could easily look different with nothing in the city having changed. Thin cells do not lie on purpose; they just cannot hold still.

LiveSlicing thins every cell4 min▶

Split the month by zone and hour of the week and the city has 262 x 168 = 44,016 possible cells. Only 30,898 hold any pickup at all. Ask how many of those hold fewer than 5 and the answer is 12,772 cells, 41.3% of the non-empty ones, yet they carry only 0.7% of the trips. Thin cells are many and light.

In Staten Island it is total: every non-empty zone-hour cell holds fewer than 5 pickups. In the Bronx, 80.0% of them do. These are the same numbers session 7 uses to decide what a publisher must withhold; tonight they answer a simpler question, which is how much of the table you could rank with a straight face.

LiveHow much a small count wobbles5 min▶

For counts of independent events, a rough rule is that a count of n moves by about one over the square root of n from one period to the next, with nothing real changing. The working, from the October numbers: 1 / square root of 49,440.4 is under 1%; 1 / square root of 100 is 10%; 1 / square root of 11.6 is about 29%. Pickups are not perfectly independent (a busy evening lifts many at once), so treat the rule as a floor on the wobble, not a ceiling.

That is why 100 is a useful line for a monthly zone count, not a magic one. Above it, a month-on-month change of a few percent might mean something. Below it, a change of a quarter can be the meter's dice. The honest move is to report the count with the flag, or with an interval: learn statistics, session b7 builds the interval properly.

Real world

Sort zones by percentage change between two months and the top and bottom of the list fill with thin zones, because their counts have the most room to move. Pick the worst of them for attention and re-measure, and they tend to look better with nothing done. Barnett, van der Pols and Dobson (2005) put it plainly: "unusually large or small measurements tend to be followed by measurements that are closer to the mean". Session 6 turns that into a warning about before-and-after claims.

Self-studyThree honest moves for a thin cell4 min▶

Pool. Coarser units hold more rows. At a threshold of 5, zone x daypart withholds 18.4% of non-empty cells and borough x hour 13.0%, against 41.3% for zone x hour. Pooling helps almost everywhere, but not in Staten Island: 95.3% of its borough-hour cells are still under 5. Changing the unit changes the story, which session 2 named and session 7 measures.

Flag, do not drop. Keep the thin zone in the table with its count and a flag. Dropping it shrinks the denominator, and session 10's break button shows what that does: an estimate that reads 0.0% error against the zones it kept and -11.1% against the city.

Say why it is thin, if you can. Few events and few observers need different fixes: the first needs patience or pooling, the second needs a different instrument. Why readings go missing in sensor feeds you own is its own subject: learn IoT data, leader session 2 covers the mechanics. Here the question is where the city is measured at all.

Part 3 · build-along

Find the thin zones in pandas, and flag them

17 min hands on

Start from session 1's files in the same empty folder, with your script kept somewhere else. Each step prints a number you can check against this page. The figure is the pipeline you are building: count, bring back the zeros, flag, and summarise by borough, with nothing dropped after the session 1 ledger.

Rebuild the kept pickups and count by zone

This repeats session 1's ledger in a few lines. You should see 3876702 pickups and only 258 zones in the counts, four short of the city.

pythonimport pandas as pd y = pd.read_parquet("yellow_tripdata_2024-10.parquet") g = pd.read_parquet("green_tripdata_2024-10.parquet") t = pd.concat([ y[["tpep_pickup_datetime", "PULocationID"]].set_axis(["t", "z"], axis=1), g[["lpep_pickup_datetime", "PULocationID"]].set_axis(["t", "z"], axis=1), ], ignore_index=True) zones = pd.read_csv("taxi_zone_lookup.csv") five = zones[zones.Borough.isin(["Manhattan", "Brooklyn", "Queens", "Bronx", "Staten Island"])].set_index("LocationID") t = t[(t.t >= "2024-10-01") & (t.t < "2024-11-01") & t.z.isin(five.index)] per = t.groupby("z").size() print(len(t), len(per))

Bring back the zeros

Reindex to the 262 zones with a fill of 0. You should see 262 rows, 4 zeros, and the zeros split Manhattan 2, Staten Island 2.

pythonper = per.reindex(five.index, fill_value=0) print(len(per), (per == 0).sum()) print(five.Borough[per == 0].value_counts())

Flag the thin zones and summarise by borough

A boolean column, not a filter. You should see Bronx 8, Brooklyn 2, Manhattan 6, Queens 11, Staten Island 20, and Staten Island's month total of 231.

pythontable = five[["Borough", "Zone"]].assign(pickups=per, thin=per < 100) print(table.groupby("Borough").thin.sum()) print(table[table.Borough == "Staten Island"].pickups.sum())

Slice by hour of the week and count the cells

Day of week times 24 plus hour gives 168 slots. You should see 30898 non-empty zone-hour cells out of 44016 possible, and 12772 of them under 5.

pythonhow = t.t.dt.dayofweek * 24 + t.t.dt.hour cells = t.groupby([t.z, how]).size() print(len(cells), 262 * 168, (cells < 5).sum())

Write the flag into the output

Save the 262-row table with the thin column and a one-line note: "Zones with fewer than 100 pickups in October 2024 are flagged; their counts are too small to rank or compare month to month." Anyone who ranks the table now has to ignore the flag on purpose.

The habit to keep. Start every zone table from the full list of zones, never from the rows that happened to exist. Flag thin cells instead of dropping them, write the threshold next to the table, and never rank, map or compare a cell whose count could move by a quarter with nothing changing in the city.

Check yourself

Three questions

A dashboard ranks zones by month-on-month percentage change in taxi pickups, and Staten Island zones fill the top and the bottom of the list. What is the most likely reason?

A count of n wobbles by roughly one over the square root of n; at 11.6 that is about 29%. Thin zones crowd both ends of any ranking by change. A reads noise as behaviour; C names a cause the data gives no evidence for.

Your groupby on pickup zone returns 258 rows, but there are 262 zones in the five boroughs. What happened?

Manhattan 2 and Staten Island 2 recorded zero pickups in October 2024. They are real zones with a real count of 0, and leaving them out shrinks every denominator built on the table.

8 of the Bronx's 43 zones recorded under 100 pickups in the month. A colleague concludes those neighbourhoods need fewer cabs. What should you say?

Recorded pickups measure supply meeting demand. Thin data marks where the measuring is thin; 311 reporting shows the same mechanism with residents as the observers. 100 is a working line for noise, not a measure of need.
