Zones, joins and the map

A trip record says where the meter was switched on as a number. Tonight that number becomes a place: real New York City taxi records, October 2024, joined to the Taxi and Limousine Commission's zone lookup and drawn as a dot map. Wrenmouth, a constructed city transport office, needs that map before it can place a single sensor. You will see which zones are not places, which zones a careless join deletes, and how the same trips tell a different story when the unit of the map changes.

Part 0

Wrenmouth's analyst asks for a map

Wrenmouth is a constructed transport office in a fictional city; its analyst practises on real New York City records. Session 1 left her with 3,876,702 in-month pickups, each carrying a PULocationID. Her manager asks a reasonable question: where are they? Answering it takes a small CSV, one join, and two decisions that look like plumbing and are really choices about what the map is allowed to say.

★ What you walk out with tonight. The zone lookup read as a table of 265 rows, of which 262 are places in the five boroughs and three are not (Newark Airport, Unknown, Outside of NYC). A join that starts from the zones, so the 4 zones with no pickups in October stay on the map as zeros instead of vanishing. LiveA zone is an area with an id3 min▶

The data dictionary defines PULocationID as the "TLC Taxi Zone in which the taximeter was engaged". The trip file holds the id and nothing else: no name, no borough, no coordinates. Those live in two separate official files, the Taxi Zone Lookup table (a CSV) and the Taxi Zones shapefile (the outlines). Until you join, every map of trip records is a bar chart of integers.

Real world

In October 2024, Manhattan's 69 zones held 88.0% of the month's 3,876,702 pickups. Manhattan is 69 of 262 zones, about a quarter of the map. A dot map makes that imbalance visible in a second; a table of ids hides it completely.

Self-studyThe files you need2 min▶

From the TLC Trip Record Data page: the October 2024 yellow and green parquet files (already on disk from session 1), the Taxi Zone Lookup table and the Taxi Zones shapefile, a zip. Download into the same empty folder, keep your scripts elsewhere, and read; nothing in them runs.

Taxi zone names and borough names are public geography. Use them freely in labels; they are the vocabulary your readers already have.

Part 1 · covers the Taxi Zone Lookup and the join

265 rows in, 262 zones kept, and which side you start from

12 min live

The lookup is small enough to read whole, and you should: 265 rows with a LocationID, a borough, a zone name and a service zone. Three rows are not places in the five boroughs. And the join that attaches them to trips has a direction, which decides whether a zone where nothing was recorded shows up as a zero or not at all.

LiveThree rows that are not places4 min▶

Read the lookup top to bottom and the borough column holds seven values, not five. Zone 1 is Newark Airport, borough EWR, outside the city. Zone 264 is "Unknown". Zone 265 is "Outside of NYC". The five boroughs hold the other 262: Manhattan 69, Queens 69, Brooklyn 61, Bronx 43, Staten Island 20.

In October, 13,169 in-month pickups carry one of the three. Session 1 dropped them with a count and a reason. Tonight's question is what the map does with them, and the answer is nothing: an id that means "missing" has no outline to fill and no centroid to plot. If it reaches the map at all, it arrives as a bar labelled Unknown, and someone will ask where Unknown is.

LiveStart the join from the zones, not the trips5 min▶

The warehouse habit is to group the trips by PULocationID, then join names on. That table has one row per zone that had at least one pickup. In October, 4 of the 262 zones had none: 2 in Manhattan and 2 in Staten Island. They are simply absent, and an absent row cannot be drawn as an empty dot.

Start instead from the 262-row lookup, left-join the counts and fill the gaps with 0. Now the table has a row for every place, and a zero is a finding: no cab engaged its meter there in a month. Check it with two numbers you can predict: the row count is 262 and the sum is 3,876,702.

Real world

Staten Island's 20 zones recorded 231 pickups in the whole month, and 2 of those zones recorded none. A trips-first join draws Staten Island with 18 dots and no gaps, which looks like sparse coverage. The zones-first join draws 20 dots, 2 of them empty rings, which shows that part of a borough is entirely unseen by this data.

Self-studyThe service zone column, and the dropoff side3 min▶

The lookup's last column, service_zone, sorts zones into "Yellow Zone", "Boro Zone" and "Airports". The two airport zones, JFK and LaGuardia, sit in Queens; JFK alone recorded 194,145 pickups in October and LaGuardia 118,732, most of Queens' 382,136. Session 5 treats them as their own stratum when placing sensors, because a zone that behaves like no other zone should not be averaged with its neighbours.

Everything tonight uses the pickup id. The dropoff id joins the same lookup the same way. If you join both, rename the lookup's columns on each side (PU_Borough, DO_Borough) so the second join does not overwrite the first.

Part 2 · covers the modifiable areal unit problem

Same trips, different units, different story

13 min live

Geographers have a name for a trap every map falls into: results computed on areas change with the size and the boundaries of the areas. Openshaw's 1984 monograph is the usual reference (reported here; we teach the mechanism with our own numbers). Take the month's pickups, cut them into zone x hour-of-week cells, then into borough x hour-of-week cells, and apply one rule: withhold any cell with fewer than 5 trips. The trips do not change. The story does.

LiveReading the two panels5 min▶

At zone x hour, 12,772 of 30,898 non-empty cells have fewer than 5 trips: 41.3% of the cells, but only 0.7% of the trips, because the small cells are small. Split by borough, the withheld share runs from 7.4% of Manhattan's cells to 80.0% of the Bronx's and 100.0% of Staten Island's.

At borough x hour, the same pickups fall into 778 cells and 101 are withheld, 13.0%. Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens and the Bronx lose nothing. Staten Island still loses 95.3% of its cells. Two analysts with the same file and the same rule would publish two different maps of the Bronx and both be correct.

Real world

Wrenmouth (constructed) wants a weekday-evening demand map for its outer districts. At zone x hour it would show the Bronx as mostly blank; at borough x hour it would show the Bronx as one healthy number. Neither map is wrong. Each answers a different question, and the unit is the question.

LiveWhat changes with the unit4 min▶

Size. Bigger units hold more trips per cell, so fewer cells fall under any threshold, and averages look steadier. The price is detail: a borough total cannot tell you which of the Bronx's 43 zones carry its 13,318 pickups.

Boundaries. Even at the same size, where the lines fall decides which trips share a cell. Taxi zones were drawn by the TLC for its own purposes; a sensor district, a council ward or a grid of squares would cut the same pickups differently. This page measures the size effect with the zones and boroughs we have; the boundary effect needs other outlines and is left as homework.

The working rule: pick the unit from the decision, say it out loud, and show at least one other unit before anyone acts on the map.

Self-studyWhy this returns in session 73 min▶

Withholding small cells is how publishers of counts protect the people behind them; practitioner session 7 treats it properly, including why it is a related practice for published counts and not the k-anonymity definition applied to person records. Tonight's point is narrower: the unit you map at decides which places get published at all. The thinnest boroughs pay first at every unit.

For the limits of k-anonymity itself, learn data access control, session b5 covers them. For honest map and chart choices in general, learn dataviz, honest charts.

Read the lookup and keep the five boroughs

Expect 265 and 262, and a borough count of Manhattan 69, Queens 69, Brooklyn 61, Bronx 43, Staten Island 20. Look at the three rows you dropped by eye; it takes ten seconds.

pythonimport pandas as pd zones = pd.read_csv("taxi_zone_lookup.csv") B = ["Manhattan", "Brooklyn", "Queens", "Bronx", "Staten Island"] five = zones[zones.Borough.isin(B)] print(len(zones), len(five)) print(five.Borough.value_counts()) print(zones[~zones.Borough.isin(B)])

Count per zone, then join from the zones side

Start from five, not from the counts. Expect 262 rows, a sum of 3876702 and 4 zeros. Then print the zeros by borough: 2 in Manhattan, 2 in Staten Island.

pythoncounts = kept.groupby("z").size().rename("pickups") zt = five.merge(counts, left_on="LocationID", right_index=True, how="left") zt["pickups"] = zt["pickups"].fillna(0).astype(int) print(len(zt), zt.pickups.sum(), (zt.pickups == 0).sum()) print(zt[zt.pickups == 0].Borough.value_counts())

Borough totals, as shares of trips and of zones

Manhattan should print 3411386 pickups on 69 zones: 88.0% of the trips on 26.3% of the zones. Staten Island: 231 pickups on 20 zones. Put the two share columns side by side; that pair is the shape of every later session.

pythonb = zt.groupby("Borough").agg(zones=("LocationID", "size"), pickups=("pickups", "sum")) b["trip_share"] = (b.pickups / b.pickups.sum() * 100).round(1) b["zone_share"] = (b.zones / b.zones.sum() * 100).round(1) print(b.sort_values("pickups", ascending=False))

Attach a centroid to every zone and draw the dots

Read the shapefile, keep one row per LocationID, take each outline's centroid in the file's own projected coordinates, and join on the id. Every one of the 262 zones should get a point. Size the dots by area, not radius, and draw zero zones as hollow rings so they stay visible.

pythonimport geopandas as gpd import matplotlib.pyplot as plt shp = gpd.read_file("taxi_zones.zip").drop_duplicates("LocationID") c = shp.geometry.centroid shp["x"], shp["y"] = c.x, c.y zt = zt.merge(shp[["LocationID", "x", "y"]], on="LocationID", how="left") print(zt.x.isna().sum()) has = zt.pickups > 0 plt.scatter(zt.x[has], zt.y[has], s=zt.pickups[has] ** 0.5 / 4) plt.scatter(zt.x[~has], zt.y[~has], s=30, facecolors="none", edgecolors="black") plt.gca().set_aspect("equal"); plt.savefig("zones.png", dpi=300)

Count the cells at two units

Make an hour-of-week key, then count non-empty cells and the share under 5 at each unit. Expect 30898 cells and 0.413 at zone x hour, 778 and 0.130 at borough x hour. Those are the two panels of Part 2.

pythonk2 = kept.merge(five[["LocationID", "Borough"]], left_on="z", right_on="LocationID") k2["how"] = k2.t.dt.dayofweek * 24 + k2.t.dt.hour cz = k2.groupby(["z", "how"]).size() cb = k2.groupby(["Borough", "how"]).size() print(len(cz), round((cz < 5).mean(), 3), len(cb), round((cb < 5).mean(), 3))

The habit to keep. Build the place table first and join the events onto it, never the other way round. Predict the row count and the sum before you run the join, and print both after. Name the unit on every map, and look at one other unit before anyone makes a decision from it.

Check yourself

Three questions

A colleague groups October's pickups by zone, joins names onto the result, and gets 258 rows. What happened?

A group-by only produces rows for values that occur. All 262 zones are in the lookup; 4 had no pickups in October. Joining from the zones side keeps them as zeros, and 262 minus 4 is exactly the 258 your colleague saw.

At zone x hour, 80.0% of the Bronx's cells fall under 5 trips; at borough x hour, none do. Which statement is right?

This is the modifiable areal unit problem: results on areas change with the size and boundaries of the areas. Neither unit is the true one. The professional move is to name the unit and show another before acting.

Your zone table has a row labelled "Unknown" with a large count. What should the map do with it?

Zone 264, "Unknown", is a value that means missing; it has no outline and no centroid. Placing it (B) or spreading it (C) invents locations. Count it, say so, and map what has a place.
