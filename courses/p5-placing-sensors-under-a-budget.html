Part 0

Forty sensors, 262 zones, one week to decide

Wrenmouth, a constructed city transport office, has a budget of 40 counting sensors. Each one counts every pickup in its zone for the month. The office places them using the first week's recorded pickups, then estimates the whole city from what the 40 zones counted. The zones and the counts are real New York City taxi records, October 2024: 3,876,702 pickups in 262 zones across five boroughs. The budget, the rules and the decision are constructed, and every Wrenmouth number below says so.

Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class 🔍 Every diagram zooms

★ What you walk out with tonight. Three placement rules you can name and code: busiest zones, spread across boroughs, and an equity floor. The greedy coverage rule, each next sensor to the borough with the most zones per sensor, N_h / (n_h + 1), and the two allocations it produces for Wrenmouth's 40 sensors: 11/9/11/6/3 spread, 11/9/10/6/4 with a floor of 4. Why the two airports are measured with certainty, as a take-all stratum. And a Python function that reproduces both allocations from the zone lookup alone.

LiveThe decision, stated plainly2 min▶

A placement rule answers two questions in order: how many sensors each borough gets, then which zones inside each borough get them. The first question is the allocation; the second is the draw. Most arguments about sensor placement are really about the first question, and most of the damage happens there too: a borough that gets zero sensors does not appear in the numbers at all.

Real world

In October 2024, Manhattan held 88.0% of the month's recorded pickups on 69 of the 262 zones. Staten Island recorded 231 pickups across its 20 zones in the whole month. Any rule that follows the trips will follow them into Manhattan, because that is where the cabs were.

Self-studyWhat a sensor is, in this model2 min▶

A Wrenmouth sensor is deliberately simple: it counts every pickup in its own zone and nothing in the zones next door. The zone shapes are shipped only as centroids, so neighbour coverage is not modelled. Real counting devices drift, drop readings and need calibrating; those mechanics live in learn IoT data, leader session 2. This session owns where the sensors are, and who that leaves unmeasured.

Part 1 · covers the three placement rules

Three rules, one budget

12 min live

The same 40 sensors, the same 262 zones, three different rules. Each looks reasonable in a meeting. Lay their allocations side by side and the difference is not a matter of degree: one rule measures two boroughs, the other two measure all five.

LiveRule 1: put the sensors where the trips are4 min▶

Rank the 262 zones by first-week pickups and take the top 40. It is the rule most teams reach for first, because it maximises what the sensors see: the busiest placement covers 88.9% of the month's trips with 15.3% of the zones. It puts 38 sensors in Manhattan and 2 in Queens, and the 2 are JFK Airport and LaGuardia Airport. Brooklyn, the Bronx and Staten Island get none.

Then Wrenmouth estimates the city by assuming every unmeasured zone looks like the average measured zone. The measured zones are the busiest in the city, so the estimate is 22,570,200 pickups against a truth of 3,876,702: +482.2%. For the outer boroughs, estimated from two airports, the error is +6,388.6%. Three boroughs have no estimate at all; they are dark.

Real world

The 40 busiest zones in the October 2024 records hold 88.9% of all pickups. That concentration is a fact about where cabs were present to be hailed (practitioner session 3), and a rule that follows it inherits it. Seeing most of the trips is not the same as seeing most of the city.

LiveRule 2: spread across boroughs4 min▶

Give each borough sensors in proportion to its zones, using the greedy coverage rule in Part 2, then draw that many zones at random inside each borough with a fixed seed. For 40 sensors the allocation is 11/9/11/6/3 (Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens, Bronx, Staten Island). Nobody is dark. The sensors now see only 11.8% of trips, and the estimate uses each borough's own average, scaled up by its own zone count.

On the canon draw (seed 1017) the city estimate is off by -25.6% and the outer boroughs by -67.8%. It is less wrong than rule 1 by a wide margin, and still wrong in a specific way: a random draw in Queens can easily miss both airports, and they are the two biggest zones in the borough.

LiveRule 3: an equity floor, then stratify4 min▶

Every borough gets at least 4 sensors first, and the rest go out by greedy coverage: 11/9/10/6/4. Inside each borough the zones are split into a busier half and a quieter half by first-week pickups, and Queens' two airports become a stratum of their own that is always measured. The sensors see 20.4% of trips. On the canon draw the city estimate is off by -17.7% (3,191,512 against 3,876,702) and the outer boroughs by +22.0%.

Per borough, rule 1 / rule 2 / rule 3: Manhattan +66.8% / -19.8% / -23.1%; Brooklyn dark / -58.4% / +8.2%; Queens +2,724.7% / -70.7% / +25.8%; Bronx dark / -34.2% / -12.8%; Staten Island dark / -42.3% / -19.9%. One draw is one draw: the next card looks at 400 of them.

Self-studyWhy seeing more trips made the answer worse3 min▶

Compare rule 1 with rule 3 directly. Rule 1 sees 68.5 more percentage points of the trips (88.9% against 20.4%). In exchange, the sensors outside Manhattan fall from 29 to 2 and the outer-borough error goes from +22.0% to +6,388.6%. More coverage of trips, less coverage of the city: that is the anti-lever practitioner session 10 lets you press.

Over 400 fresh placements at the same settings, the spread rule misses the city by 21.3% on average and its worst borough by 120.5%; the equity rule misses the city by 11.4% and its worst borough by 47.2%. These are results of a constructed office's rules run on real counts, not a property of any real city's network. Who gains and who loses under each rule is practitioner session 9.

Part 2 · covers greedy coverage and the take-all stratum

Where the next sensor goes

13 min live

Rules 2 and 3 need a way to turn "in proportion to zones" into whole sensors. Greedy coverage does it one sensor at a time: give the next sensor to the borough with the most zones per sensor after it lands, N_h / (n_h + 1), where N_h is the borough's zone count and n_h its sensors so far. Repeat until the budget is gone. It never gives a borough a fraction of a sensor, and it never forgets a small borough for long.

LiveGreedy coverage, sensor by sensor5 min▶

Start from zero under rule 2. The first sensor goes to Manhattan (69 / 1), the second to Queens (also 69 / 1, a tie decided by list order), then Brooklyn (61) and the Bronx (43). The fifth goes back to Manhattan, now at 69 / 2 = 34.50. Staten Island's first sensor is the 12th placed, when 20 / 1 = 20.00 beats Manhattan's 69 / 4 = 17.25. Forty steps later the allocation is 11/9/11/6/3.

The ratio is the number of zones each sensor would stand for once it lands. Giving the next sensor to the borough where that number is biggest keeps the zones-per-sensor as even as whole sensors allow. It is the same idea as allocating a sample in proportion to stratum size, done one unit at a time so the total is exact.

Real world

Staten Island recorded 231 pickups in October 2024, 0.006% of the month, on 7.6% of the city's zones. Allocate by trips and it gets nothing. Allocate by zones and it gets 3 of 40 under rule 2, and the floor lifts it to 4.

LiveThe floor, and the tie it moves3 min▶

Rule 3 starts every borough at 4 (20 sensors), then runs the same greedy rule for the other 20. The figure shows the last step: Manhattan and Queens tie at 69 / 11 = 6.27, and the tie goes to Manhattan. Under rule 2 Queens also won its 11th sensor, as the 40th. Under rule 3 Staten Island's floor sensor has already used that budget, so Queens ends on 10.

A floor is a statement that every borough must be measurable, even at the cost of fewer sensors where the zones are many. It is a policy choice, and Wrenmouth's floor of 4 is constructed. Write it in the allocation memo with the reason, not in a config file nobody reads.

LiveAirports as a take-all stratum4 min▶

Inside a borough, rule 3 does not draw from all zones at once. If Queens' 10 sensors were drawn at random from 69 zones, whether the estimate included JFK and LaGuardia would be decided by the seed, and the estimate would swing wildly between draws. So the two zones whose service zone is "Airports" form a take-all stratum: both get a sensor, and their counts go into the estimate as counts, with no expansion and no sampling error.

The remaining 67 Queens zones split into a busier half (34) and a quieter half (33) by first-week pickups, and greedy coverage splits the other 8 sensors 4 and 4. Each half is then expanded by its own zone count. The take-all only fires when the borough's budget covers the airports plus two, so each half still gets at least one sensor.

Real world

JFK Airport was the busiest zone in the city in October 2024, with 194,145 pickups; LaGuardia Airport recorded 118,732. They are unlike every other Queens zone. Under rule 1, they are also the only two sensors outside Manhattan, which is how two airports came to stand for four boroughs.

Self-studyWhy greedy counts zones, not trips3 min▶

Run greedy coverage with recorded trips as the size of each borough and you rebuild rule 1 in slow motion: Manhattan's 88.0% share would claim almost every sensor. The point of the rule is to measure places, so the size is the number of places. Trips come back in only inside a borough, to rank the busier and quieter halves, where they decide how zones are grouped and never whether a borough is measured.

An allocation is not an estimate. Turning the 40 counts into a city total, and saying how far off it might be, is rung 4 of the ladder in practitioner session 10; the interval mechanics are in learn statistics, session b7.

Part 3 · build-along

Allocate 40 sensors, then let the engine draw

17 min hands on

You write the allocation yourself in Python, from the zone lookup alone, and check it against Wrenmouth's canon: 11/9/11/6/3 and 11/9/10/6/4. Then you split Queens into strata from the first week's pickups. The seeded draws and the scoring run in the engine on this page, which uses the same rules on the same real counts.

Count zones per borough from the lookup

Keep the five boroughs only. You should see Manhattan 69, Brooklyn 61, Queens 69, Bronx 43, Staten Island 20: the 262 zones of practitioner session 2.

pythonimport pandas as pd zones = pd.read_csv("taxi_zone_lookup.csv") order = ["Manhattan", "Brooklyn", "Queens", "Bronx", "Staten Island"] five = zones[zones.Borough.isin(order)] sizes = [int((five.Borough == b).sum()) for b in order] print(sizes, sum(sizes))

Write greedy coverage, and make the tie rule explicit

Each next sensor goes to the borough with the largest N_h / (n_h + 1). On a tie the borough listed first wins; the -h in the key says so out loud. Spread from zero should print 11/9/11/6/3.

pythondef greedy(sizes, start, K): n = list(start) for _ in range(K - sum(start)): open_ = [h for h in range(len(sizes)) if n[h] < sizes[h]] best = max(open_, key=lambda h: (sizes[h] / (n[h] + 1), -h)) n[best] += 1 return n print(greedy(sizes, [0] * 5, 40))

Add the equity floor

Start every borough at 4, then run the same function. You should get 11/9/10/6/4. Print the ratios before the last step and find the 6.27 tie.

pythonfloor = 4 start = [min(floor, s) for s in sizes] print(greedy(sizes, start, 40)) n = greedy(sizes, start, 39) print([round(s / (k + 1), 2) for s, k in zip(sizes, n)])

Split Queens into strata from the first week

Use the kept frame from practitioner session 1 (columns t and z). The airports leave first; the rest rank by first-week pickups, busier half rounded up. You should see 2, 34 and 33, and greedy splits the remaining 8 sensors 4 and 4.

pythonw1 = kept[kept.t < "2024-10-08"].groupby("z").size() q = five[five.Borough == "Queens"].copy() q["w1"] = q.LocationID.map(w1).fillna(0) air = q[q.service_zone == "Airports"] rest = q[q.service_zone != "Airports"].sort_values(["w1", "LocationID"], ascending=[False, True]) busier = rest.head((len(rest) + 1) // 2) print(len(air), len(busier), len(rest) - len(busier)) print(greedy([len(busier), len(rest) - len(busier)], [0, 0], 10 - len(air)))

Check yourself

Three questions

Wrenmouth (constructed) puts its 40 sensors on the 40 zones with most first-week pickups. They see 88.9% of the month's trips. What is the problem?

Trip coverage and city coverage are different denominators: rule 1 measures 15.3% of zones and leaves three of five boroughs with no sensor. C fails too: at 80 sensors the busiest rule still leaves two boroughs dark.

Greedy coverage, mid-allocation: Manhattan has 4 sensors on 69 zones, Brooklyn 4 on 61, the Bronx 4 on 43. Which borough gets the next sensor?

The rule compares zones per sensor once the next sensor lands, N_h / (n_h + 1). B describes a floor, which has already been applied. C is rule 1 again, the one that leaves boroughs dark.

Why does rule 3 measure both Queens airports with certainty instead of drawing them at random with the other Queens zones?

Both airports are Queens zones, so A is wrong. Seeing more trips is rule 1's logic (B). Take-all removes the two most extreme zones from the random part, which is what stops the estimate swinging between draws.
