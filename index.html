Learn Smart City Data with Phoebe

A warehouse row is a business event someone meant. A city row exists only where a sensor, a vehicle or a form happened to be, about residents who never agreed to be measured. Wrenmouth, a constructed transport office in a fictional city, runs through every session; it practises on real New York City taxi records for October 2024, 3,876,702 pickups in 262 zones, 88.0 percent of them in Manhattan. Its obvious plan, 40 counting sensors on the busiest zones, sees 88.9 percent of trips, covers 15.3 percent of the city and misses the boroughs outside Manhattan by +6,388.6 percent. Six leader sessions on the decision, ten practitioner sessions on the build. Nothing here is investment or procurement advice, and no vendor or product is named.

If you come from a data team

Leader session 6 in three columns. What a conventional analyst, engineer or scientist already has that is worth more here, what is new, and what is actively dangerous.

Carries over

  • Profiling before aggregating, with a ledger of every dropped row and its reason
  • Joins on a lookup table, and treating an id that means "unknown" as missing
  • Rates with a stated denominator, and asking "coverage of what"
  • Reproducible pipelines: seeds, and a reference implementation that agrees to the digit

Must learn

  • Who writes a city row: meters, sensors and residents, each with its own blind spot
  • Censored demand: a recorded pickup is demand that met supply
  • Stratified placement and estimation, with intervals checked for coverage
  • Re-identification risk and small-cell suppression on published counts

Must unlearn

  • "No rows means no demand": it can mean no cab, no sensor or no report
  • "Measure where the activity is": 88.9 percent of trips, three boroughs dark
  • Error computed only where you measured: 0.0 percent reported, -11.1 real
  • "Aggregated means anonymous": four location points identify 95 percent of people
